# The Never-Say List

Every line in this document was said on a real recorded mortgage cold call. They are
taken verbatim from the four transcripts in `07-teardowns/`.

The originator on those calls is genuinely skilled — better than most people with five
years in the business. That is exactly why this document exists. **Skill is what makes
bad habits contagious.** A new LO listens to those calls, hears how smooth they are, and
copies everything, including the parts that carry real regulatory exposure.

Read this before you build your script, not after.

---

## How to use this

Each entry gives you:

- **The line** — verbatim
- **Why it's a problem** — the specific rule
- **What to say instead** — a replacement that keeps the sales function intact

The replacements are not softer. Several are *more* effective. Honest language usually
outperforms evasive language on the phone, because evasion is audible.

---

# Tier 1 — Say these and you may lose your license

---

## 1.1 Misrepresenting where the lead came from

> ❌ **"I'm just following up on a recent inquiry that was submitted."**
> ❌ **"We had received your information this morning from one of our correspondent
> lenders."**
> ❌ **"Following up on an inquiry you have submitted."**

**The problem.** In the transcripts, no inquiry was submitted. These were credit-bureau
trigger leads. The line is a **false statement of material fact about the origin of the
contact** — deceptive under UDAAP, and a state MLO conduct violation in most
jurisdictions. It also fails the TSR requirement to truthfully disclose the purpose of
the call.

It is also now **operationally obsolete** — trigger leads were banned effective
March 5, 2026 (`02-the-trigger-lead-ban.md`).

**Instead — when you have a genuine opt-in lead:**

> ✅ *"You filled out a request on [site] on [date] about [purpose]. I know that was a
> while back — is that still on your radar or has that ship sailed?"*

**Instead — when you're calling from public data or a purchased non-inquiry list:**

> ✅ *"I'll be straight with you — you didn't call me. I'm a mortgage broker here in
> [area], and I'm calling homeowners in [neighborhood] because [specific, true reason].
> Do you have thirty seconds for me to tell you why, and then you can tell me to get
> lost?"*

That second one converts better than the fake-inquiry opening. Honesty delivered with
confidence is a pattern interrupt — nobody expects it, so it buys you the thirty seconds.

---

## 1.2 Doubling down when they call you on it

> Borrower: *"I didn't send anything to you."*
> ❌ **"Right, so we're a mortgage broker. We work with over 50 different lenders. So
> that is how we got your information. So, you know, you sent us information."**

**The problem.** This is Call 2, and it's the most dangerous exchange in all four
transcripts. She correctly denied submitting anything; the response asserted the false
fact a second time, more explicitly. A regulator reading that transcript sees knowing
deception, and a recording exists.

**Instead — when a borrower denies the inquiry, believe them immediately:**

> ✅ *"You know what, you may well be right, and I apologize — these lists come from
> aggregators and they're not always accurate. Let me just be direct about why I'm
> calling instead."*

You lose nothing. You gain enormous credibility, because you did the thing she did not
expect: you backed down honestly. From there you have a real conversation.

---

## 1.3 Continuing to pitch after a clear refusal

> Borrower: *"No, I'm not interested."* … *"I'm not interested, okay?"* … *"Listen to
> what I'm saying. I'm not interested. I don't need anything. I don't want a mortgage
> from you guys. Okay?"*
> ❌ *[pitch continues]*

**The problem.** FCC rules require honoring revocation of consent through **any
reasonable means**. "I'm not interested" repeated three times, plus "I don't want a
mortgage from you guys," is a revocation by any standard. Continuing is a TCPA
violation, and in the mini-TCPA states it is $500–$20,000 of exposure sitting on a
recording.

**The standard to hold:**

| What they said | What you do |
|---|---|
| "Not interested" (first time, reflexive) | **One** reframe is defensible |
| "Not interested" (second time, clear) | Stop |
| "Take me off your list" / "stop calling" | Stop **immediately**, first instance |

**Instead:**

> ✅ *"Understood, I'll take you off our list right now. Sorry to have bothered you —
> have a good one."*

Then log it. Then move on. There are more names on the list.

---

## 1.4 Promising a rate you have not priced

> ❌ **"I know for a fact I can do a lot better than a 7.2."**
> ❌ **"I have a few lenders in mind that I believe are going to offer far lower than a
> 6%."**
> ❌ **"We'll definitely knock that down for you with no problem at all."**

**The problem.** Said to a borrower with a **581 FICO**, a **forbearance**, and a
recently disclosed **employment gap** — before any credit pull, before any pricing. This
is an unsubstantiated promise of a material loan term. UDAAP-deceptive, a state MLO
violation, and a violation of federal mortgage advertising rules (Reg N / the MAP Rule)
if it appears in any marketing.

It is also the kind of thing that produces the ugliest possible outcome: the borrower
cancels their other loan, you can't deliver, and they lose their house or their earnest
money.

**Instead — you can be confident without promising:**

> ✅ *"7.2 is high for a 30-year fixed even with a 581 — that tells me they either priced
> it lazily or there's something in the file driving it. I can't promise you a number
> before I pull credit and run it through my lenders, and I won't. What I can promise is
> that I'll run it through all fifty and tell you honestly whether I can beat it or not.
> If I can't, I'll tell you that too and you should stay where you are."*

That is **stronger**, not weaker. "I'll tell you if I can't beat it" is the single most
trust-generating sentence available to you on a cold call, because no one else says it.

# Tier 2 — Say these and you'll get in trouble with compliance

---

## 2.1 Fabricated authority and manufactured urgency

> ❌ **"There are times my upper management will match another lender's offer if I
> request the match. I know I can get the approval to honor this quote, but my upper
> management will only do so if we lock today."**

This one is from the Amir Syed video, taught as a "takeaway close."

**The problem.** If there is no such policy, it is deceptive — fabricated authority plus
fabricated urgency, both material to the decision. And *"I know I can get the approval"*
followed by *"only if we lock today"* is internally contradictory in a way a sharp
borrower will hear.

**The honest version — which is genuinely available to you as a broker:**

> ✅ *"Let me be transparent about how my pricing works. I have discretion within my comp
> structure to sharpen this, and I'd rather use it to keep your business than lose it
> over an eighth. Send me their Loan Estimate — not a screenshot of a rate, the actual
> LE — and I'll tell you straight whether I can beat it. If I can, I'll do it today. If
> I can't, I'll tell you to take their deal."*

Real, specific, and it gets you the LE, which is what you actually needed.

> **On urgency generally:** urgency is fine when it's *real*. "Rates moved 18 basis
> points yesterday and my lock desk reprices intraday" is a true statement in this
> market. Manufactured urgency is what gets punished. Use the real thing — there's
> plenty of it.

---

## 2.2 Quoting after saying there's no credit pull

> ❌ **"It's no credit pull or anything like that, so it's just a free quote."**
> *(followed by discussion of specific rates and payments)*

**The problem.** Not inherently deceptive — but "free quote" invites the borrower to
treat an estimate as an offer. Without credit, you don't know FICO, and FICO drives
LLPAs, which drive rate. Any number you produce could move by a full point once credit
comes in.

**Instead:**

> ✅ *"I'm not pulling credit for this — nothing hits your report. Which means what I give
> you is an estimate based on what you've told me. If your score comes in where you think
> it is, the real number lands close. If it doesn't, it moves, and I'll tell you the
> moment it does. Fair?"*

You have now inoculated yourself against the "you said 6.5 and now it's 7.1" conversation
that kills deals at week three.

---

## 2.3 Technically-true, materially-misleading qualification claims

> ❌ **"We take scores as low as a 500."**
> ❌ **"The minimum is actually going to be a 500."**

**The problem.** FHA's floor is 500 — with **10% down**, and virtually no wholesale
lender actually buys 500-score paper without heavy overlays. A borrower hears "I
qualify." That gap between technically true and practically true is precisely what
UDAAP's "likely to mislead a reasonable consumer" standard captures.

**Instead:**

> ✅ *"FHA's floor is technically 500, but I want to be straight with you — below about
> 580 you're looking at 10% down and a very short list of lenders who'll actually touch
> it. At 620 and up the whole market opens. Where do you think you're sitting?"*

You just taught them something true, which makes you the expert in the conversation.

---

## 2.4 Implying you can match an unmatched rate

> Borrower has a **3.1% VA loan.**
> ❌ **"What is your current interest rate, so we could try to get you as close to that
> as possible?"**

**The problem.** The market is ~6.8%. There is no path to 3.1%. Suggesting you'll try
sets an expectation you cannot meet — deceptive in effect even if not intended, and
commercially suicidal. When you come back with 6.9% on a cash-out, the borrower feels
misled, and they're right.

**Instead — reframe from rate to purpose, immediately:**

> ✅ *"You've got a 3.1% — that's a phenomenal rate and I'm not going to pretend I can
> touch it. Nobody can; the market's at about 6.8%. So the real question isn't rate, it's
> whether pulling $120,000 out is worth what it costs you to give up that 3.1%. Sometimes
> it is and sometimes it absolutely isn't. Want me to show you both ways — a cash-out
> that replaces it, and a second lien that leaves your 3.1% completely alone?"*

That answer is the difference between an amateur and an advisor. See
`../3-GO-DEEPER/product-6-second-liens-heloc.md`.

---

## 2.5 Self-serving advice dressed as neutral guidance

> ❌ **"That process tends to be a little bit more annoying because obviously PennyMac is
> going to want to maintain your business. They're going to try not to send it over and
> just not comply."**

**The problem.** This steers the borrower toward cancelling a competitor's appraisal
based on a speculative accusation of non-compliance. Appraisal transfer is a real,
regulated process; alleging another lender will obstruct it, without knowledge, is
disparagement in service of your own commission.

**Instead:**

> ✅ *"Appraisals can transfer between lenders — it's allowed, though it takes some
> back-and-forth and not every lender accepts a transfer. Cleanest path is usually to let
> theirs finish and see if my lender will take it. Don't cancel anything on their side
> until we know my numbers actually beat theirs — I don't want you out an appraisal fee
> for nothing."*

Protecting the borrower's money at your own short-term expense is the most powerful
trust move in the entire call. Use it.

# Tier 3 — Bad habits that cost you deals

Not illegal. Just amateur.

---

## 3.1 Talking over the borrower

> Borrower: *"...and he was like, well, hang on, let me look because I haven't been late
> in the last two years—"*
> ❌ *"Still with me, Michael? Are you there?"*

The borrower was mid-story, explaining a late payment — the most important underwriting
fact in the call. That is the moment to be silent. Amir Syed's fifth technique is the
power of silence, and it was available here and not used.

**Instead:** say nothing. Let them finish. Then: *"So walk me through that — what
happened?"*

---

## 3.2 Filler that erodes authority

> ❌ *"Okay, beautiful."* (used ~11 times in a single call)
> ❌ *"Not a problem."* / *"Gotcha."* (used constantly)

Once, it's warm. Eleven times, it's a verbal tic, and it signals you're on rails rather
than listening. Vary acknowledgments, and sometimes just move to the next question.

---

## 3.3 Interrogating instead of consulting

Call 1 asks nineteen consecutive fact-gathering questions with almost no explanation of
why any of them matter. It feels like a form, because it is one.

**Instead — bracket your discovery with purpose:**

> ✅ *"I'm going to ask you maybe six things, and then I'll be able to tell you whether
> this is even worth your time. Some of it's nosy — that's the job. Ready?"*

And explain the *why* on the sensitive ones: *"I ask about disability income because on a
VA loan it's tax-free, which means underwriting grosses it up 25% — it counts for more
than the number suggests."*

Now you're an advisor. Same questions, completely different call.

---

## 3.4 Accepting a vague next step

> Borrower: *"If I don't answer, shoot me a text and I'll call you when I get done."*
> ❌ *"That's not a problem."*

That's not an appointment; it's a hope. See `../5-SALES/07-follow-up.md`.

**Instead:**

> ✅ *"Let's do this — I'll text you the numbers by 2:00 so you have them in writing. Then
> I'll call you at 4:15 when you're done with the job. If 4:15 doesn't work, tell me now
> and I'll make it whenever does. What's better, 4:15 or 5:00?"*

---

## 3.5 Letting the borrower leave without a reason to stay

Call 4's borrower has unfiled tax returns and a 30-day mortgage late. The call ends with
*"I'll look into it and call you back."* There is nothing for the borrower to do, and
nothing to look forward to.

**Instead, always assign homework:**

> ✅ *"Two things before we hang up. One, ask your CPA when your returns will actually be
> filed — that's the gate on the whole thing. Two, pull your last two months of business
> bank statements. If the returns aren't ready, there's a bank-statement program that
> ignores tax returns entirely and I want to price that for you. Can you have those by
> Thursday?"*

Now the borrower is invested, and you have a real reason to call back.

# The one-page version

Print this.

**Never:**
1. Claim they inquired when they didn't
2. Repeat a lead-source claim after they deny it
3. Keep pitching after a second clear refusal
4. Promise a rate before credit and pricing
5. Invent manager approvals or lock deadlines
6. Say "as low as 500" without the real-world caveat
7. Imply you can approach a sub-4% legacy rate
8. Tell them a competitor will refuse to comply
9. Talk over an underwriting disclosure
10. Hang up without a specific time and a specific task

**Always:**
1. Name, company, NMLS, recorded line — up front and true
2. Announce the purpose of the call honestly
3. Stop at "take me off your list"
4. Say "I'll tell you if I can't beat it" — and mean it
5. Explain *why* you're asking the nosy questions
6. Protect their money before your commission
7. Get silent when they start talking about their situation
8. Book the next contact to a specific time
9. Log every revocation immediately
10. When compliance and the close conflict, compliance wins

---

## What you must be able to do

- Spot a deceptive lead-source claim in any script handed to you
- Convert a rate promise into a confident, compliant commitment
- Distinguish real urgency from manufactured urgency
- Recognize the moment a sales objection becomes a revocation

**Self-check:** A borrower says *"another lender quoted me 6.25."* You haven't pulled
credit. What do you say?

> *"That's a strong number — if it's real, take it. Do me one favor before you do: send
> me their Loan Estimate, not a screenshot. Half the time that 6.25 has two points of
> discount buried in it and the actual cost is higher than what I'd charge you. If their
> LE is genuinely better, I'll tell you to sign it. If it isn't, I'll show you exactly
> where the difference is. Either way you'll know."*
