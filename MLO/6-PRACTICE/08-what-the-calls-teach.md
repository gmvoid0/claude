## The five patterns

### Pattern 1 — Nobody asked what the money was for

| Call | Cash requested | Purpose established? |
|---|---|---|
| Michael | $120,000 | ❌ Never asked |
| Paula | $200,000 | ❌ Never asked |
| Tyler | $49,000 | ⚠️ "Pay off debt" — no balances, no rates |
| Fasani | $100,000 | ⚠️ "Expand the business" — asked, then dropped |

**Half a million dollars of requested cash across four calls, and not one call
established what the money was actually for in usable detail.**

This is the single most consistent failure in the set, and it cascades:

- No emotional driver → nothing to tie down in positioning
- No purpose → can't select the right product
- No urgency → no reason to act today
- No suitability analysis → potential UDAAP "abusive" exposure

**The fix is one question:** *"What's the money for?"* Then follow it with *"what happens
if you don't get it?"*

### Pattern 2 — Every borrower had a legacy rate, and it was never handled

| Call | Current rate | Market (Aug 2026) | Rate-term refi viable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Michael | **3.1% VA** | ~6.8% | ❌ No |
| Paula | **2.3%, 15-yr** | ~6.8% | ❌ Absolutely not |
| Tyler | 4.2–4.3% | ~6.8% | ❌ No |
| Fasani | 4.5% FHA | ~6.8% | ❌ No |

**Zero of four.** And in Call 1 the originator said: *"What is your current interest rate,
so we could try to get you as close to that as possible."*

That framing is guaranteed to fail. The borrower will feel misled when the callback comes
in at 6.9%, and they'll be right.

**The fix — say it out loud, immediately:**

> *"You've got a three-one. I can't beat that, nobody can, the market's at about
> six-eight. So this isn't a rate call. The question is whether we can get you what you
> need without touching it."*

That's the highest-trust sentence available to you in this market, and it appears in none
of the four calls.

### Pattern 3 — The second lien was never mentioned. Not once.

| Call | Equity | Legacy rate | Second lien correct? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Michael | ~$442,000 | 3.1% | ✅ Almost certainly |
| Paula | ~$800,000 | 2.3% (15-yr) | ✅ Almost certainly |
| Tyler | ~$175,000 | 4.25% | ⚠️ Maybe — ARM likely better |
| Fasani | ~$142,000 | 4.5% | ⚠️ Bank statement is the gate |

**In four calls with a combined ~$1.5 million in equity behind legacy-rate first
mortgages, the product designed for exactly this situation was never raised.**

A cash-out refinance destroys a 2.3% or 3.1% rate on the *entire* balance. A second lien
leaves it completely intact and prices market rate on the new money only.

**This is the defining product of the 2026 market.** Not knowing it isn't a sales problem
— it's a product-knowledge problem that costs the borrower real money.

### Pattern 4 — Every borrower revealed something material that was passed over

| Call | What they said | Why it mattered | What happened |
|---|---|---|---|
| Michael | 30-day late from an expired autopay card | **Potentially documentable** | Interrupted mid-story |
| Michael | 10% VA disability | **Funding fee exemption** + grossed-up income | Collected, never used |
| Paula | *"Maybe I'll be dead by the time it's due"* | Suitability of a 30-year term | Laughed past |
| Paula | $6,000/yr insurance on a $950k home | Real monthly savings available | Noted, ignored |
| Tyler | *"I'm selling in two years"* | **ARM + lender credits**, not a 30-yr fixed | Acknowledged, then sold rate anyway |
| Tyler | $13,000 forbearance | Most likely deal-killer | Treated as a line item |
| Fasani | Returns not filed | **Bank-statement program** | Never mentioned |
| Fasani | Late payment from a fraud freeze | **Potentially documentable** | One sentence |

**Every single call contained the key to itself, spoken aloud by the borrower, and passed
over.**

This is not a knowledge gap. This is a **listening** gap — the originator was running a
script and collecting fields rather than reacting to what was said.

## What the originator is genuinely good at

This must be said clearly, because it's why the calls are worth studying at all.

**1. Tonality under pressure.** Paula was openly hostile and he never got defensive, never
matched her energy, never rushed. That is a real, hard-won skill and most people don't
have it.

**2. The "second option on the table" reframe.** Low-commitment, non-adversarial, doesn't
ask the borrower to leave anyone. It's the best recurring line in the set and it works on
three of four calls.

**3. Forward motion.** Every call ends with the borrower agreeing to something. He never
lets a call die passively.

**4. The text bridge.** Acknowledging that the borrower is being spammed and offering a
recognition token is genuinely thoughtful and it should be standard practice.

**5. Reverse-engineering the competitor's structure** (Call 3). Reconstructing PennyMac's
loan structure live from partial information, and confirming it, is the single most
impressive moment across all four calls. That's real expertise.

**6. Getting the value from the borrower.** *"You'd know the home better than I would —
what do you think it'd appraise for?"* Flattering, disarming, and it makes the borrower
own the number.

**7. Genuine warmth.** The VA service acknowledgment in Call 1 is real, well-placed, and
the borrower's tone visibly changes.

**8. Persistence.** Converting Paula from three refusals into full discovery is
extraordinary as pure persuasion — which is exactly why the legal caveat has to be so
loud.

> **The uncomfortable truth: he is a better *talker* than most people with five years in
> the business, and a worse *advisor* than someone who's read the product guides once.**
>
> That's a fixable gap, and it's the entire thesis of this curriculum. Sales skill without
> product knowledge produces exactly these calls — smooth, engaging, and pointed at the
> wrong solution.

## The eight rules these calls produce

Everything above compresses to this. This is the practical output of the teardowns.

1. **Say the rate truth first.** If they hold a 3%, tell them immediately that nobody can
   beat it. Then pivot.
2. **Always ask what the money is for.** Then ask what happens if they don't get it.
3. **Know the second lien cold.** It's the answer for most legacy-rate borrowers.
4. **Shut up when they disclose.** Credit events, life events, timelines. That's the call.
5. **Match the product to the horizon.** Two years means ARM and lender credits. Never
   sell a 30-year fixed to someone who told you they're leaving.
6. **A documentable credit event is a different animal.** Fraud, bank errors, expired
   autopay — ask for the paperwork.
7. **Never promise a rate before you've priced it.** "I'll tell you if I can't beat it" is
   stronger anyway.
8. **End with time + task + channel.** Every call, no exceptions.

## How to use this

**Before your first block:** read the eight rules above. That's it.

**Weekly:** pick one teardown and re-read it, then listen to one of your own calls with
that specific pattern in mind. Are *you* asking what the money is for? Are *you* handling
the legacy rate?

**When you lose a deal:** find the pattern here that matches and go to the linked module.

**Run `/call-score` on your own transcripts.** The scorecard used in these teardowns is
the same one the skill applies. Score yourself against the same standard — and note that
the highest score in this set was **49/110** from someone who is genuinely skilled. The
bar is not high. Being a genuine advisor with a script that tells the truth would put you
well ahead of the calls in this set.
