# Teardown 1 — Michael (VA, 3.1%, $120k cash out)

**The call:** ~7 minutes. Trigger lead. Borrower engages fully, gives complete
information, agrees to a callback.

**Surface verdict:** a good call. Warm, efficient, thorough.

**Actual verdict:** the originator collected enough to price a loan he cannot sell, never
found out what the money was for, and missed the one product that would have worked.

---

## The borrower

| Fact | Value |
|---|---|
| Current balance | ~$108,000 |
| Estimated value | $550,000 |
| **Equity** | **~$442,000** |
| Current rate | **3.1%, 30-year fixed VA** |
| Payment | ~$1,300 + ~$250 escrow |
| Cash wanted | $120,000 |
| Income | $72,000 base, W-2 |
| Employment | **30 years**, same employer, becoming an owner |
| Veteran | Yes, **10% VA disability** (~$148/mo, tax-free) |
| Credit event | One 30-day mortgage late ~6 months ago, expired debit card on autopay |

**This is an outstanding borrower.** Enormous equity, 30 years of job stability, a
veteran, one explainable late. He should be easy to close.

---

## What went right

Credit where it's due — several things here are genuinely good.

### The name-confirm open
> *"Hello, Michael?"*

Correct. Personal, confirms identity, gets a response.

### The competitor reframe
> *"That's kind of the main reason we're reaching out, just showing they're not able to
> offer you the best deals on the market. Just calling to put a second option on the
> table for you."*

Structurally excellent. Doesn't attack the competitor, doesn't ask him to leave anyone,
lowers the stakes to "a second option." **This is the transcripts' best recurring move.**

### The broker explanation
> *"We're a wholesale broker, so I partner with over 50 different lenders to try to get
> you the best deals on the market."*

Clear, short, and it explains why he's worth talking to. Most borrowers don't know what a
broker is.

### The VA acknowledgment
> *"Definitely wanted to take a step back and thank you for your service. I know our
> veterans don't get the recognition that they do deserve."*

Genuine, well-placed, and the borrower's tone visibly warms afterward. **Real rapport.**

### The text bridge
> *"I'll send you a text message from my personal cell phone number. It'll come from a
> 248 area code... so that way you're able to distinguish it's me."*

Excellent. Acknowledges the borrower's actual experience of being spammed and gives a
recognition token. Steal this.

### Getting the value from the borrower
> *"You would obviously know the home better than I would. What do you think it would
> appraise for in today's market?"*

Smart. The borrower supplies the number, which means he owns it, and the flattery is
disarming.

## What went wrong

### ❌ 1. The rate promise that cannot exist

> *"What is your current interest rate? So we could try to get you as close to that as
> possible."*
> *"It's like 3-1, I think."*

**The market is ~6.8%.** There is no path to 3.1%. None. Not with fifty lenders, not with
five hundred.

By asking that question in that framing, the originator set an expectation he was
guaranteed to fail. When he calls back with 6.9%, Michael will feel misled — and he'll be
right.

**This is the single largest error in the call**, and it's the error that most defines
amateur mortgage sales in 2026.

**What should have happened, immediately:**

> *"A three-one? Okay, stop me right there — that's a phenomenal rate and I want to be
> straight with you: I can't beat it and neither can anybody else. The market's at about
> six-eight. So this isn't a 'lower your rate' call, and if that's what you were hoping
> for I'd rather tell you now."*
>
> *"What I can do is get you the $120,000 without touching that three-one at all. Want me
> to show you how?"*

That answer builds more trust in fifteen seconds than the rest of the call combined.

### ❌ 2. The product that was never mentioned

Michael has **$442,000 in equity** and a **3.1% rate on a $108,000 balance**.

A cash-out refinance would replace his entire $108,000 balance at ~7% to give him
$120,000. He'd be paying roughly 7% on $228,000 instead of 3.1% on $108,000.

**A fixed second lien** would leave the 3.1% completely intact and put $120,000 behind
it. He keeps the phenomenal rate on the existing balance and pays market rate only on the
new money.

The rough comparison (illustrative — run real numbers in
`../3-GO-DEEPER/underwriting-5-math-in-your-head.md`):

| | Cash-out refi | First + second |
|---|---|---|
| Rate on original $108k | ~7% | **3.1% (untouched)** |
| Rate on new $120k | ~7% | ~market second rate |
| First mortgage | Destroyed | **Preserved** |

**The second lien is almost certainly the right answer, and it was never raised.**

This is not a small miss. It's the difference between a loan officer and an advisor.
See `../3-GO-DEEPER/product-6-second-liens-heloc.md`.

### ❌ 3. Nobody asked what the $120,000 was for

> *"About how much cash was it that you were looking to receive?"*
> *"$120."*
> *"About $120. Okay. Beautiful."*

**And that's it.** The call moves on to escrow.

$120,000 is a lot of money. Is it debt? A business? A second property? Medical? A
divorce? College? Each one implies a different product, a different urgency, and a
different close.

Without it, there is no emotional driver in the entire call — nothing to tie down in
Stage 5, nothing to close on. He collected a number and lost the reason.

**This is the most consistent failure across all four transcripts.**

### ❌ 4. Interrupting the underwriting disclosure

> Michael: *"...he was like, well, hang on, let me look because I haven't been late in
> the last two years. And they were trying to get that late. They called it a late
> payment off of my — it wasn't really my fault. It was my fault, but it wasn't my fault.
> Do you know what I mean?"*
> LO: *"Yeah, yeah, I got you."*
>
> [moments later] *"Still with me, Michael? Are you there?"*

Michael was mid-disclosure on **the single most important underwriting fact in the call**
— a 30-day mortgage late, six months ago, caused by an expired debit card on autopay.

That late payment determines which lenders will touch this file. And crucially: **an
autopay failure caused by an expired card is potentially documentable.** A letter of
explanation plus bank records showing the card expiry can sometimes be worked with
underwriting.

The right move was silence, then:

> *"Take your time — walk me through exactly what happened. This matters more than
> anything else we've talked about."*

Instead: *"Okay, okay, beautiful. It shouldn't be a problem."*

**"It shouldn't be a problem" is a promise he has no basis to make.** He doesn't know the
FICO, hasn't seen the credit report, and hasn't checked a single lender's overlay.

### ❌ 5. The VA disability income was collected and never used

> *"Are you receiving any VA disability at the moment?"* — Yes, 10%, ~$148/month.

Then it's dropped entirely.

**VA disability income is tax-free, which means underwriting can gross it up — commonly
by 25%.** That $148 counts as roughly $185 for qualifying. Small, but it's free DTI room.

More importantly: **10% VA disability makes the borrower exempt from the VA funding
fee.** On a VA loan that is a direct, substantial saving — real money, and a genuinely
impressive thing to know.

The originator collected the fact and did nothing with it. Saying it out loud would have
been the "diagnose" moment the call never had:

> *"Ten percent disability — that's actually significant here. It means you're exempt
> from the VA funding fee, which on a loan this size is real money most people don't know
> about. And because it's tax-free income, underwriting grosses it up. You're in better
> shape than you think."*

### ❌ 6. Discovery was an interrogation

Nineteen consecutive factual questions with almost no explanation of why any mattered.
No frame at the top, no "why" attached to the sensitive ones.

See `../5-SALES/03-discovery.md`.

### ❌ 7. Filler

*"Okay, beautiful"* appears roughly eleven times. *"Not a problem"* and *"gotcha"*
throughout. It reads as being on rails rather than listening.

### ❌ 8. The next step dissolved

> Michael: *"If I don't answer, shoot me a text message and I'll call you when I get
> done, or if you want to shoot me a text message when you got it all put together, try
> calling me, but if I don't answer, I'll call you back."*
> LO: *"That's not a problem."*

The borrower just took control of the follow-up and made it conditional on his own
initiative. That deal is now a coin flip.

**What should have happened:**

> *"Let's do this. I'll text you the numbers by 2:00 so you've got them in writing, then
> I'll call you at 4:15 when you're done with the job. If 4:15 doesn't work, tell me now
> and I'll make it whenever does. What's better — 4:15 or 5:00?"*

## The call that should have happened

> *"Michael? Hi, my name is [Name] with [Company], NMLS [ID], on a recorded line. I'll be
> straight with you — you didn't call me. I'm a mortgage broker and I'm reaching out to
> homeowners about equity options. Give me thirty seconds and then tell me to get lost if
> you want."*
>
> *[gets the 3.1%]*
>
> *"Stop me right there. A three-one is phenomenal — I can't beat it, nobody can, market's
> about six-eight. So I'm not calling to lower your rate. What I want to know is whether
> there's a way to get you what you need without touching it. **What were you hoping to
> do with the money?**"*
>
> *[LISTENS. Full discovery on purpose, timeline, and consequence.]*
>
> *"Okay. So here's what I'd do. You've got about $442,000 in equity and a rate nobody
> should ever give up. Instead of refinancing that $108,000 into today's market, we leave
> your first mortgage completely alone and put a fixed second behind it for the $120,000.
> Your three-one stays exactly where it is, forever."*
>
> *"Two other things you should know. You're at 10% VA disability — that makes you exempt
> from the VA funding fee, which is real money. And that disability income is tax-free,
> so underwriting counts it for more than the number looks like."*
>
> *"Now tell me about that late payment. Take your time — this is the part that actually
> matters."*

Same borrower. Same facts. Entirely different call.

## The three lessons

1. **When a borrower has a legacy rate, say so immediately.** It is the highest-trust
   move available and it prevents the entire call from being built on a false premise.
2. **Always ask what the money is for.** Without it you have a quote request, not a sale.
3. **Shut up when they start explaining a credit event.** That's the most valuable
   information in the call and it only comes once.
