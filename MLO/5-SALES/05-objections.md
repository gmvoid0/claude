# Objection Mastery

The reference document you'll come back to most. Techniques first, then the full matrix.

---

## The frame

**An objection is information.** Someone with zero interest hangs up — they don't argue
with you about rates or explain their situation. Engagement, even hostile engagement, is
engagement.

**But hold the legal line clearly.** "Objections are buying signals" is a claim about
psychology, not a permission slip:

| This is an objection — work it | This is a revocation — stop |
|---|---|
| "I'm already working with someone" | "Take me off your list" |
| "Your rate's too high" | "Stop calling me" |
| "I need to think about it" | "I'm not interested" *(2nd clear time)* |
| "Now's not a good time" | "Do not call this number again" |

See `../4-COMPLIANCE/01-cold-calling-law.md`. Every coach in this industry blurs this.
Don't.

---

## Prevention beats handling

The best objection is the one that never happens. If you hear the same objection on 60%
of calls, that's a **script defect upstream**, not an objection-handling problem.

| Objection you keep getting | Fix it here instead |
|---|---|
| "I need to ask my spouse" | Discovery: *"Is anyone else part of this decision?"* |
| "How did you get my number?" | Open honestly in the first 10 seconds |
| "I need to think about it" | Weak Diagnose/Position — nothing to think *with* |
| "Your rate's too high" | You presented before you positioned |
| "I don't have time" | No Frame — you never bounded the call |

## The five techniques

### 1. The tie-down
Collect micro-agreements so the big yes is consistent with what they've already said.

> *"I completely understand — I have a personal policy never to push a refinance that
> doesn't make sense. Just so I'm clear: at the start you said the goal was to save $500
> a month and clear the high-interest cards, which is about $6,000 a year — roughly a
> month's salary back in your pocket. Is that still important to you?"*

Reaffirming their own stated goal makes backing out feel like self-contradiction. The
power comes from **their words, not yours** — which is why you take notes in discovery.

### 2. The reframe
Shift focus from the objection to the larger picture. Never argue the objection directly.

> *"Totally makes sense. Just curious — if a slightly lower rate came with higher closing
> costs, a longer processing time, and a lender who put your earnest money at risk by
> missing the contingency date, is that still the best option?"*

You didn't dispute that lower rates are good. You added variables. Their conclusion moves
on its own.

### 3. The preemptive strike
Raise the objection before they do. Highest-leverage technique in the system.

> *"Before we dive in — my priority is making sure the math works. The benefit has to
> clearly outweigh the cost, and if we should wait, I'll tell you to wait. Fair?"*

You've defused "you're just trying to sell me" before it forms. Trust up, tension down.

**Build a preemptive strike for your three most common objections and put them in your
Frame.**

### 4. The boomerang
Turn the objection into the reason to act.

> *"I want to wait for rates to drop."*
> *"I get it. Here's what I'd want you to know: for every 1% rates drop, roughly five
> million more buyers re-enter the market. The people who win aren't the ones who waited
> for the drop — they're the ones who got in first and refinanced into it. You said the
> $500k price point with 5% down puts the payment where you need it. If the payment works
> now, does the rate actually change the decision?"*

⚠️ **Sourcing caution.** The "five million buyers per 1%" figure comes from the source
video and is widely repeated. **Verify it before you use it with a borrower**, and if you
can't source it, use the qualitative version — falling rates bring buyers back and
increase competition, which is uncontroversially true. Never cite a statistic you can't
back up; when a borrower asks for the source, "I heard it somewhere" destroys everything
you built.

### 5. Silence
> *"I need time to think about it."*
> *"I understand. What else can I give you to help your thinking?"*
> **[silence]**

What comes after the silence is the real objection.

**The escalation, when you need directness:**

> *"Can I have permission to be honest? The last thing I want is to call you every day to
> check how your thinking is going when really you're just not excited about this — you
> end up ghosting me, it's awkward, and it wastes both our time. What's actually holding
> you back? Be blunt. Honesty saves us both."*

Delivered warmly, this works remarkably often. Delivered aggressively, it's a hang-up.
Tonality is the whole difference.

# The Objection Matrix

Format: **the objection** → *what's really behind it* → the response.

---

## A. Brush-offs (first 15 seconds)

### A1. "I'm not interested."
*Reflex, before they know what it is. Not yet a considered no.*

> *"Totally fair — you don't know what this is yet. Ten seconds and then you can decide:
> I'm a mortgage broker, I work with about fifty lenders, and I'm calling homeowners in
> [area] about [specific true reason]. If that's not you, I'll let you go. Is it?"*

**Stop after one attempt if they repeat it.**

### A2. "How did you get my number?"
*Suspicion. Answer completely honestly — this is a trust checkpoint.*

> *"Fair question. You filled out a request on [site] on [date] — it came to us through
> their network. If that's not something you remember doing, tell me and I'll take you
> off the list right now, no problem."*

Offering to remove them is what makes this credible. Most people won't take it.

### A3. "Now's not a good time."
*Usually true.*

> *"Of course — I'm calling out of the blue. Two options: give me sixty seconds now and
> you'll know if it's worth a real conversation, or tell me a better time and I'll call
> then. Which is easier?"*

### A4. "Take me off your list."
**Revocation. Stop.**

> *"Absolutely — done. Sorry to have bothered you."*

Log it immediately.

### A5. "Is this a sales call?"
> *"It is. I'm a mortgage broker and I'm calling about [specific reason]. Want the
> thirty-second version, or should I let you go?"*

Never dodge this. Dodging it is a TSR problem and everyone can hear it.

## B. Competitor objections

### B1. "I'm already working with someone."
*The default state of every good lead. Expect it on most calls.*

The transcript version — worth studying, it's good:

> *"That's actually the main reason we're reaching out. Just putting a second option on
> the table."*

Better:

> *"Good — you should be. I'm not calling to get you to leave them. One question: have
> you locked yet?"*
>
> **If not locked:** *"Then nothing's lost by having one number to compare. If theirs is
> better I'll tell you to stay — I mean that. If mine's better you'd want to know. Two
> minutes?"*
>
> **If locked:** *"Then stay put — breaking a lock rarely works out. When are you closing?
> I'd like to check in after, because a lot of people leave money on the table on the
> next one."* *(You just turned a dead lead into a future one.)*

### B2. "I've already settled on an offer, we're just closing."
> *"Congratulations, that's the hard part. Out of curiosity, what rate did you land?"*
> **[listen]**
> *"That's — [honest reaction]. Look, if you're a week from closing I'm not going to blow
> that up over an eighth. But if you're two weeks out and there's real money in it, would
> you want to know?"*

### B3. "Their rate is lower than yours."
> *"Could well be. Do me one favor before you sign: send me their Loan Estimate — the
> actual form, not a screenshot of a rate. Half the time that number has a point and a
> half of discount buried in it and the real cost is higher. If their LE genuinely beats
> mine, I'll tell you to sign it. If it doesn't, I'll show you exactly where the
> difference is."*

**Always ask for the LE.** It's a standardized federal form, it's comparable line by
line, and asking for it signals you're not afraid of the comparison.

### B4. "My credit union always has the best rates."
> *"Sometimes they do. Worth knowing the mechanism though — a credit union holds the loan
> on its own balance sheet, so it prices for its own risk appetite. I'm sending your file
> to fifty lenders who compete for it. Different mechanism, different outcome. Might be
> them. Let's find out."*

### B5. "I'll just go back to my current servicer."
> *"You can, and they're counting on it — retention departments exist because it works.
> They know you probably won't shop. What did they quote you?"*

## C. Rate and price

### C1. "What's your rate?" *(asked in the first minute)*
**The most important objection in mortgage sales.**

> *"I'll get you a real number, I promise — and I don't want to give you a fake one. Rate
> depends on your score, your equity, the loan purpose, and occupancy. Anyone who fires a
> number at you before knowing those is guessing, and it'll change. Give me four
> questions and I'll give you a number I can actually stand behind. Fair?"*

Then Amir's reframe:

> *"And one thing so I'm shopping for the right thing — are you after purely the lowest
> rate, or the best overall financing strategy?"*

### C2. "Your rate is too high."
> *"Compared to what?"*
> **[silence — you need the comparison to respond to]**

Then diagnose. Is it a different product, different term, discount points, an old quote,
a rate they saw on a TV ad? Usually it's not comparable and they don't know that.

### C3. "I want to wait for rates to drop."
See the boomerang above. Plus the second lien angle:

> *"Here's a version most people don't know about. Instead of refinancing your 3.1% into
> today's market, we leave that first mortgage completely alone and put a second behind
> it for the cash you need. You keep the 3.1% on the big balance. When rates drop, we
> consolidate. Want me to price both?"*

**This is the single most valuable reframe in the 2026 market.** See
`../3-GO-DEEPER/product-6-second-liens-heloc.md`.

### C4. "The closing costs are too high."
> *"They're real money and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. Three ways to handle them:
> pay them, roll them into the loan, or I take a slightly higher rate and the lender
> credits them back — you pay nothing at closing. Which one fits how long you're staying
> in the house?"*

Turns a complaint into a choice. And the "how long are you staying" question is the
correct analytical frame anyway.

### C5. "Can you do better?"
> *"Maybe. Let me be straight about how it works: I have some discretion within my comp
> structure, and I'd rather use it to keep your business than lose it over an eighth.
> Send me the competing Loan Estimate and I'll tell you honestly whether I can beat it or
> whether you should take theirs."*

**Never** invent a manager approval or a fake deadline
(`../4-COMPLIANCE/06-the-never-say-list.md`).

## D. Trust and stalling

### D1. "I need to think about it."
Silence technique first, then the honesty escalation. See above.

### D2. "I need to talk to my spouse."
*Either legitimate or a soft no. You should have prevented this in discovery.*

> *"Absolutely, they should be part of it. Two things: what do you think their first
> question will be? And would it be easier if I got you both on the phone for ten minutes
> so I can answer it directly instead of you having to relay it?"*

Getting both on the phone roughly doubles close rates on joint decisions.

### D3. "Send me something in writing."
*Often a polite exit — but sometimes real.*

> *"I'll send it right now. Before I do, so I'm sending the right thing — is there a
> specific number you're trying to compare, or do you want the full breakdown?"*
> *[listen]*
> *"Great. I'll have it to you in ten minutes, and I'll call you at 3:00 to walk through
> it — those forms are dense and I don't want you guessing at page two. Does 3:00 work?"*

**Never send without booking the follow-up call.** That's how leads die.

### D4. "I've never heard of your company."
> *"Fair. Look me up while we're talking — NMLS Consumer Access, my ID is [number].
> You'll see my license, my states, and my history. Honestly, you should do that with
> anyone who asks you for a Social Security number over the phone."*

### D5. "How do I know this isn't a scam?"
> *"You don't yet, and you're right to be careful. Here's what a real one looks like: I'm
> going to give you my NMLS ID, my company's license number, and a callback number you
> can verify. I'm not going to ask you for a Social today. And I'm never going to ask you
> to wire money anywhere. Verify me, then call me back."*

Never be offended by this question. Being unbothered is the proof.

## E. Qualification and credit

### E1. "My credit's bad."
> *"Tell me what 'bad' means to you — people say that about a 640 and about a 540 and
> they're completely different conversations. What's your rough number?"*

Then be honest about the tiers (`../4-COMPLIANCE/06-the-never-say-list.md` — no "as low
as 500" without the caveats).

### E2. "I had a late payment."
*Extremely common. Two of the four transcripts.*

> *"How long ago, and was it the mortgage or a credit card?"*
> **[listen fully — do not interrupt]**
> *"Okay. One 30-day mortgage late in the last twelve months narrows which lenders will
> touch it but doesn't kill it. Was it a genuine miss or an autopay/bank problem? Because
> if there's documentation from the bank, sometimes that's explainable to underwriting."*

Both transcript borrowers had late payments caused by payment-method failures — an
expired debit card and a fraud-related account freeze. **Both are potentially
documentable.** In both calls the story got cut off.

### E3. "I'm self-employed, I write everything off."
> *"That's the classic problem — you're a great business owner and it makes you look poor
> on paper. Two paths. If the returns show enough, we go conventional and it's cheapest.
> If they don't, there's a bank-statement program that ignores tax returns entirely and
> qualifies you off deposits. Costs a bit more in rate. Which do you think your returns
> support?"*

**Call 4 was this borrower and this product was never mentioned.** See
`../3-GO-DEEPER/product-5-non-qm.md`.

### E4. "I don't have money for a down payment."
> *"Okay — let's find out what you actually need. VA is zero down if you've served. USDA
> is zero down in eligible areas. FHA is 3.5%, and it can be gifted from family. Some
> conventional programs go to 3%. Which of those might apply to you?"*

### E5. "I was denied before."
> *"By who, and what did they say the reason was?"*
> **[listen]**
> *"A denial from one lender is one lender's overlay, not the whole market — I've got
> fifty and they don't all have the same rules. Do you still have the adverse action
> letter? It'll tell us exactly what to fix."*

## F. Product and structure

### F1. "I don't want to restart my 30 years."
*Legitimate and usually ignored. Amir's swap-out close.*

> *"Good instinct — most people don't think about that. You're three years in, so instead
> of resetting to thirty, let's look at a twenty-five. You still cut the monthly payment
> and you don't give back the three years you've already paid. Want to see both side by
> side?"*

### F2. "I don't want to put my credit card debt on my house."
*Thoughtful, and sometimes correct.*

> *"That's a real concern and I'm not going to dismiss it. Two things have to be true for
> this to make sense. One, the blended rate has to actually drop — turning 24% debt into
> 7% debt is the whole point. Two, and this is the one that matters: you have to actually
> close the cards. If you run them back up, you've made your situation worse and I'd
> rather you didn't do it at all. Can you close them?"*

Honest, protects them, and massively increases trust.

### F3. "Isn't a HELOC better?"
> *"Might be. HELOC is variable, so the payment moves — good if you're paying it off fast,
> risky if you're not. A fixed second is a fixed payment. What's your plan for paying it
> back?"*

### F4. "What's a broker? Why not go to a bank?"
> *"A bank sells you its own money and has one rate sheet. I send your file to about
> fifty lenders and they compete. Sometimes the bank wins and I'll tell you when it does.
> But you only see one price at a bank, and you never find out what the other forty-nine
> would have said."*

## G. Hostile

### G1. "Stop calling me!" / "This is harassment."
**Revocation. Stop immediately.**

> *"Understood — I'm taking you off right now. Sorry to have bothered you."*

No pitch. No last word. Log it.

### G2. "You people are scammers."
> *"I get it — you're getting a lot of these calls and some of them are junk. I'm a
> licensed broker, my NMLS is [number], and you can verify me in thirty seconds. But if
> you'd rather I just let you go, say the word and I'm gone."*

Offering to leave defuses hostility better than defending yourself.

### G3. "I'm on the Do Not Call list."
> *"Then I apologize — let me get that corrected on our end right now. I'll take you off
> and make sure it doesn't happen again."*

**Do not argue about EBR exemptions with a consumer.** Even if you're technically
compliant, arguing DNC law with an angry homeowner is how you become a defendant. Remove
them, log it, escalate to compliance internally.

## H. The one nobody prepares for

### H1. "You sound young."
> *"I am. Which is why I check everything twice and answer my phone at nine at night.
> Ask me anything you want and see if I know the answer."*

Never apologize for it. Convert it to a work-ethic claim and then prove competence
immediately.

### H2. "How long have you been doing this?"
> *"[Honest answer]. I'll tell you what that means practically: you're not going to be
> number sixty on my list. If you want to talk to someone with twenty years, I work with
> people here who have it and I'll bring them in on the call. But you'll never wonder
> whether I'm paying attention to your file."*

**Never lie about experience.** It's discoverable, and the honest version is genuinely
persuasive.

## Using this document

Don't memorize it. Do this instead:

1. **Pick your five most common objections this week.** Write your responses in your own
   words — words that sound like you.
2. **Say them out loud fifty times.** Not read. Said.
3. **Drill them** with `/cold-call-drill`.
4. **After every lost call**, find which objection killed it and come back here.

A memorized script delivered word-for-word sounds memorized. What you want is the
*structure* internalized so deeply that your own words come out under pressure.

## What you must be able to do

- Distinguish an objection from a revocation, instantly
- Deploy all five techniques by name
- Handle "what's your rate?" without quoting
- Handle "I'm already working with someone" in both locked and unlocked cases
- Redirect a low-rate legacy borrower to a second lien
- Ask for the competing Loan Estimate every single time

**Self-check:** *"I've got a 2.75% and I'm not giving it up, but I need $50,000 for a
kitchen."* What do you say?

> *"You shouldn't give it up — I wouldn't let you. Refinancing that into today's market
> would cost you far more than the kitchen is worth. What we do instead is leave your
> first mortgage completely untouched and put a fixed second behind it for the $50k. Your
> 2.75% stays exactly where it is. Want me to price that?"*
