# The Call Arc

Every good mortgage call moves through the same eight stages in the same order. Skip one
and you'll feel the call go wrong without knowing why.

This is the skeleton. Discovery, tonality, objections, and closing each get their own
document; this is how they fit together.

---

## The arc

```
 1. OPEN          Earn the next 30 seconds
 2. FRAME         Say why you're calling and what happens next
 3. DISCOVER      Find the problem. Longest stage by far.
 4. DIAGNOSE      Tell them something true they didn't know
 5. POSITION      Frame the solution against their stated goal
 6. PRESENT       Numbers — and only now
 7. CLOSE         Ask for a specific next action
 8. LOCK THE NEXT Specific time, specific task, specific channel
```

**Time budget on a 12-minute call:**

| Stage | Time | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Open + Frame | 45 sec | 6% |
| **Discover** | **6 min** | **50%** |
| Diagnose + Position | 2.5 min | 21% |
| Present | 2 min | 17% |
| Close + Lock | 45 sec | 6% |

**Half the call is discovery.** If you're talking more than the borrower, you're doing it
wrong. The transcripts get this roughly right — that's the best thing about them.

---

## Stage 1 — Open

**Goal:** earn the next thirty seconds. That's all. You are not selling anything yet.

You need four things out fast, and truthfully:

1. Who you are
2. What company
3. Why you're calling — **honestly**
4. A question that's easy to answer

Full scripts in `10-script-book.md`. The mechanics of the
first fifteen seconds in `09-the-first-15-seconds.md`.

**The most common failure:** pitching in the open. The borrower has no reason to care yet
and every reason to end the call.

---

## Stage 2 — Frame

**Goal:** tell them what this call is and what it isn't. Thirty seconds. Almost nobody
does it, and it dramatically reduces resistance for the rest of the call.

> *"Here's what I'd like to do — ask you maybe six questions so I understand what you're
> actually trying to accomplish. Then I'll either tell you I can help and show you the
> numbers, or I'll tell you to stay where you are. Either way you'll know in about ten
> minutes. Fair enough?"*

Why this works:

- **It sets the agenda**, so questions don't feel like an interrogation
- **It pre-frames a possible "no"**, which lowers their guard immediately
- **It's a small yes** — the first tie-down of the call
- **It bounds the time**, defusing "I don't have time for this"
- **It's a preemptive strike** against "you're just trying to sell me something"

This is also where Amir's preemptive strike lives:

> *"Before we dive in — my priority is making sure the math actually works. The benefit
> has to clearly outweigh the cost, and if the answer is that you should wait, I'll tell
> you to wait. That okay?"*

---

## Stage 3 — Discover

**Goal:** find the actual problem, and the emotion underneath it. This is the call.

Full treatment in `03-discovery.md`. The arc-level rules:

- **Half the call.** Minimum.
- **Purpose before facts.** Find out *why* before you collect *what*. The transcripts
  invert this — they lead with balance and value, which turns discovery into data entry.
- **Explain your nosy questions.** "I ask about disability income because on a VA loan
  it's tax-free, so underwriting grosses it up 25% — it counts for more than the number
  looks like."
- **Shut up when they start talking about their situation.** Especially about credit
  problems, job changes, or family. That's the real information.
- **Take notes.** You will quote them back to themselves in Stage 5, and it's devastating.

**The question that outperforms everything else:**

> *"What would a successful outcome look like to you?"*

Victor's question. It's better than "what are you looking for" because it asks them to
describe the *destination*, not the *product*. And whatever they answer is the thing you
close on later.

---

## Stage 4 — Diagnose

**Goal:** tell them one true thing they didn't know. This is where you stop being a
telemarketer.

Every borrower has a wrong belief. Find it and correct it gently:

| Their belief | The diagnosis |
|---|---|
| "I need to lower my rate" *(has 3.1%)* | "You can't. Nobody can. The real question is whether the cash is worth giving that up — or whether we leave it alone and put a second behind it." |
| "I'll just wait for rates to drop" | "Maybe. Understand that when they do, five million buyers come back and start bidding against you." |
| "7.2% is what I qualify for" | "With a 581, that's not crazy — but it's on the high side even for that. Something in the file is driving it, and I'd like to find out what." |
| "My credit union always has the best rate" | "Sometimes. They also hold the loan, so they price for their own balance sheet. I price against fifty lenders competing for it. Different mechanism." |
| "Consolidating debt into my mortgage is bad" | "Sometimes it is — you're turning 5-year debt into 30-year debt. Depends entirely on the blended rate and whether you actually close the cards." |

**This is the moment you become the expert.** One correct, useful, non-obvious statement
changes the entire register of the conversation. It's worth preparing five of them for
your most common borrower types.

---

## Stage 5 — Position

**Goal:** frame the solution against **their words**, not your product.

The mechanism is the **tie-down** — make them reaffirm the goal they already stated, then
attach the solution to it:

> *"So earlier you said the goal was $120,000 to expand the trucking business, and that
> the monthly payment mattered more than the rate. Is that still right?"*
> *[wait for yes]*
> *"Okay. Then here's how I'd structure this..."*

You've now got a **yes** on the record and you're solving *their* stated problem, not
pitching *your* product. Backing out now means contradicting themselves.

**Position against the goal, never against the competitor.** "We're cheaper than
PennyMac" is a weak frame. "This gets you the $120k and keeps your payment under $2,000,
which is what you said mattered" is a strong one.

---

## Stage 6 — Present

**Goal:** deliver numbers. **Not before now.**

Rules:

- **One recommendation, one alternative.** Three options paralyze people.
- **Payment first, rate second.** They feel payment. Rate is abstract.
- **Total cost, not just monthly** — you're an advisor, not a payment-hider.
- **Say the number and stop talking.** Do not narrate through the silence.
- **Never present a number you can't stand behind after credit.** See
  `../4-COMPLIANCE/06-the-never-say-list.md`.

> *"Here's what it looks like. $185,000 loan, 30-year fixed, payment lands at $1,610 with
> taxes and insurance. That's about $50 more a month than you pay now, and it puts
> $49,000 in your hand and clears the forbearance. Rate's 6.9."*
> *[stop]*

Payment, benefit, delta, rate — in that order. Then silence.

---

## Stage 7 — Close

**Goal:** ask for a specific next action.

The four closes are in `06-closing.md`. Arc-level rules:

- **Always ask.** The most common reason a call doesn't close is that nobody asked.
- **Ask for something small and specific.** Not "do you want to do this" — "let's get
  credit pulled so I can firm this up. Do you have five minutes now?"
- **Then be silent.**
- **Assume, don't beg.** "Here's what happens next" beats "so, um, what do you think?"

---

## Stage 8 — Lock the next contact

**Goal:** never end a call without a specific time, a specific task, and a specific
channel.

This is the most commonly skipped stage and it's where the transcripts leak the most
deals. Call 1 ends with *"if I don't answer, shoot me a text and I'll call you when I get
done."* That's not a next step, that's a wish.

**The three elements:**

| Element | Bad | Good |
|---|---|---|
| **Time** | "I'll call you back later" | "I'll call you at 4:15" |
| **Task** | *(none)* | "Pull your last two bank statements before then" |
| **Channel** | "I'll reach out" | "Text from a 248 number so you know it's me" |

**Credit where due — the transcripts do one thing here really well:**

> *"I'll send you a text message from my personal cell phone number. It'll come from a
> 248 area code. I'm sure you're getting quite a few calls, so that way you're able to
> distinguish it's me when I call back."*

That is genuinely excellent. It acknowledges the borrower's real experience (they're
getting spammed), it gives them a recognition token, and it makes the callback expected
rather than intrusive. **Steal this.** Just attach it to a specific time.

## The whole arc on one page

| # | Stage | Goal | Signature move |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Open | Earn 30 seconds | Honest reason + easy question |
| 2 | Frame | Set the agenda | "Six questions, then I'll tell you yes or no" |
| 3 | Discover | Find the real problem | "What would a successful outcome look like?" |
| 4 | Diagnose | Say one true unexpected thing | Correct their wrong belief |
| 5 | Position | Attach solution to their words | Tie-down on their stated goal |
| 6 | Present | Deliver numbers | Payment → benefit → delta → rate → silence |
| 7 | Close | Ask for a next action | Assume it, make it small, then shut up |
| 8 | Lock | Secure the next contact | Time + task + channel |

## Diagnosing a call that went wrong

| Symptom | Stage that failed |
|---|---|
| Hung up in 10 seconds | 1 — Open |
| "Why are you asking me all this?" | 2 — no Frame |
| You quoted and they vanished | 3 — no Discovery, or 6 too early |
| "Let me think about it" | 4/5 — no Diagnose, weak Position |
| "Your rate is too high" | 6 before 5, or you sold rate not cash flow |
| They agreed then ghosted | 8 — no specific next contact |
| Great call, no application | 7 — you never asked |

**Use this table on every call you lose.** Over a month it will show you your one
recurring structural leak, which is worth more than any script.

## What you must be able to do

- Name all eight stages in order
- State what percentage of the call is discovery
- Explain why Frame reduces resistance
- Deliver a presentation in the payment-first order
- Close every call with time + task + channel

**Self-check:** You quoted a rate in minute two and the borrower said "let me think about
it" and disappeared. Which stages failed?

> *2, 3, 4, 5, and 6. You skipped Frame, never discovered the actual goal, never
> diagnosed anything, had nothing to position against, and presented into a vacuum. The
> rate wasn't the problem — the rate was the only thing you gave them, so it became the
> only thing they could evaluate.*
