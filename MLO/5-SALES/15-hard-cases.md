# Hard Cases

The calls that break new LOs. Each one has a correct handling — and for two of them, the
correct handling is to stop.

---

## 1. The hostile borrower

**What it sounds like:** anger, sarcasm, accusations of scamming, profanity.

### First — classify it

This is the fork that matters more than any technique:

| They're saying | Classification | Response |
|---|---|---|
| "You people are scammers" | **Venting.** Hostile, but engaged. | Workable |
| "Stop calling me" / "take me off" | **Revocation.** | **Stop. Immediately.** |
| "This is harassment" | **Revocation.** | **Stop. Immediately.** |
| "I'm on the Do Not Call list" | **Treat as revocation.** | **Stop. Apologize. Log it.** |

Get this wrong in the aggressive direction and you have a TCPA claim on a recording.
`../4-COMPLIANCE/01-cold-calling-law.md`.

### If it's venting

**Do not defend yourself. Do not match energy. Drop your volume and slow down.**

> *"I hear you — you're getting hammered with these calls and most of them are junk. I
> get it."*
>
> **[pause]**
>
> *"I'm a licensed broker, my NMLS is [number], and you can verify me in about thirty
> seconds. But honestly, if you'd rather I just let you go, say the word and I'm gone —
> no hard feelings."*

**The offer to leave is what defuses it.** They're braced for a fight. Not getting one
resolves the hostility faster than any argument. A meaningful share of these turn into
real conversations, because you did the one thing nobody else does.

**If they take the exit — take it.** Cleanly, warmly, immediately. Never a parting shot.

### The Call 2 lesson

Paula was hostile *and* said "I'm not interested" three times. The originator kept
pitching and got her into full discovery.

**Commercially impressive. Legally the most dangerous exchange in all four transcripts.**

The lesson isn't "never work through hostility" — it's that **hostility and revocation
are different things**, and you must classify before you respond. Paula gave both
signals. The revocation is the one that governs.

## 2. The gatekeeper

Less common in consumer mortgage than in B2B, but real — spouses, adult children,
assistants.

**The wrong move:** trying to get around them. They will remember, and they have veto
power over the entire decision.

**The right move:** treat them as a decision-maker, because they usually are.

> *"Hi — is Michael around?"*
> *"He's not, can I take a message?"*
>
> *"Sure — and actually, you might be the better person to ask. My name's [Name], I'm a
> mortgage broker with [Company]. Michael filled out a request about pulling some cash
> out of the house back in [month]. Do you know if you two ever got that handled?"*

**"You might be the better person to ask"** works because it's often true and always
flattering. In household financial decisions the person answering the phone is frequently
the one who actually decides.

**Never:** be evasive about why you're calling, pretend to be a friend, or imply an
existing relationship. All three destroy the deal and are TSR problems.

## 3. The competitor-locked borrower

**What it sounds like:** *"I've already settled on an offer, everything's done except
closing."* (Call 3.)

### The diagnostic question

> *"Congratulations — that's the hard part. What rate did you land?"*

Then classify:

| Situation | Your move |
|---|---|
| **Locked, closing in under 2 weeks** | **Leave it alone.** Build the future relationship. |
| **Locked, 3+ weeks out, rate is genuinely bad** | Worth one honest conversation |
| **Not locked** | Fully in play |
| **Rate reflects a real credit issue** | Be honest that you may not beat it |

### If it's late-stage — the long game

> *"Then stay put. Breaking a lock this close to closing usually costs more than it
> saves, and I'm not going to talk you into something that risks your closing date."*
>
> *"Do me one favor though. Put my number in your phone, for two reasons. One — if
> anything goes sideways in the next two weeks, and sometimes it does, call me. I've
> rescued files that fell apart at the last minute. Two — rates are projected to come
> down toward the low sixes by year end. When that happens, whoever's doing this loan
> probably won't call you. I will."*

**You just converted a dead lead into a rescue option and a future refinance**, and you
did it by telling them not to switch. That call generates more long-term revenue than
most of the ones you "win."

### If it's genuinely in play

> *"Send me their Loan Estimate — the actual form, not a screenshot of a rate. If theirs
> is genuinely better I'll tell you to sign it. If it isn't, I'll show you exactly where
> the difference is."*

### What Call 3 got wrong

Three things:

1. **"I know for a fact I can do a lot better than a 7.2"** — to a 581 FICO borrower with
   a forbearance, before any credit pull. An unsubstantiated promise of a material term.
   `../4-COMPLIANCE/06-the-never-say-list.md`.
2. **Advising him to cancel PennyMac's appraisal**, with a speculative accusation that
   they'd refuse to comply. Self-serving advice that could cost the borrower a paid
   appraisal.
3. **Ignoring what he actually said.** *"I'm selling this house in two years, so the 7.3
   doesn't really scare me."* He told you his real decision criterion. The answer wasn't
   "I'll save you money over two years" — it was **an ARM**, which prices lower precisely
   because he's not keeping it 30 years. Nobody offered it.

## 4. The unqualified borrower

You'll find out mid-call that this can't be done — credit far too low, no verifiable
income, no equity, unfiled returns with no bank-statement path.

**Do not pretend. Do not "run some numbers" to end the call politely.**

That's what Call 4 does — the borrower has unfiled tax returns and a 30-day mortgage
late, and the call ends with "let me run these numbers for you." There were no numbers to
run. The deal was gated on the returns and nobody said so.

### The right handling — become the person who told them the truth

> *"Michael, I'm going to be straight with you because I'd rather do that than waste
> three weeks of your time. As things stand today, I can't get this done. Here's exactly
> why: [specific reason]."*
>
> *"But here's what would change that. [Specific, actionable steps.] If you do those
> three things, call me and we'll do this. Want me to write them down and send them to
> you?"*

**Then follow up on the timeline.** A borrower you turned down honestly and then coached
becomes one of your most loyal clients — and one of your best referral sources — because
you're the only person in the industry who was straight with them.

### The right handling for Call 4 specifically

> *"Here's the real situation. Until those returns are filed, conventional is off the
> table — underwriting needs them and there's no way around it. So two questions. When is
> your CPA actually filing? And second, pull your last twelve months of business bank
> statements, because there's a program that ignores tax returns entirely and qualifies
> you off deposits. It costs a bit more in rate, but it closes now instead of in March.
> Which sounds better?"*

That's a real answer. `../3-GO-DEEPER/product-5-non-qm.md`.

## 5. The ghost

Great call, clear commitment, then silence.

**Understand what happened first.** A ghost usually means one of:

- They got a better offer and are avoiding the conversation
- They got cold feet and are embarrassed
- Something went wrong in their life
- **They never really committed and you misread the call**

That last one is the most common, and it's diagnostic: if you're getting ghosted a lot,
your close is producing polite agreement rather than genuine commitment
(`06-closing.md`).

**The sequence:** run the standard cadence
(`07-follow-up.md`), then the file-closing message at day 14.

> *"Michael — I don't want to be the guy who keeps calling, so I'm going to close out
> your file. If you went another direction, totally fine — no hard feelings at all. Just
> let me know so I stop bugging you. And if something changed, tell me and I'll pick it
> right back up."*

**This gets responses when nothing else does**, because it releases them from the
awkwardness. A meaningful share come back with "no no, I still want to do this."

## 6. The borrower who knows more than you

**Early in your career this will happen and it will be uncomfortable.** They'll ask about
LLPA grids, MBS pricing, buying down with discount points, DSCR ratios.

**The only correct response:**

> *"I don't know that off the top of my head. Let me find out and call you back today
> with the actual answer instead of guessing at it."*

Then **actually do it, same day.**

**Never bluff.** They will catch you, and the moment they do, everything you've said
becomes suspect. Meanwhile "I don't know, I'll find out" — followed by a same-day answer
— is genuinely impressive, because almost nobody does it.

Your youth and inexperience are not the liability you think they are. **Bluffing is.**

### If they ask directly how long you've been doing this

> *"[Honest answer]. Here's what that means practically: you're not going to be number
> sixty on my list. If you want someone with twenty years, I work with people here who
> have it and I'll bring them onto the call. But you will never wonder whether I'm paying
> attention to your file."*

Never lie about experience. It's discoverable, and the honest version is genuinely
persuasive.

## 7. The emotional disclosure

Mid-discovery, they tell you something heavy — a job loss, an illness, a divorce, a death,
a business failing.

**What most new LOs do:** rush past it, awkwardly, back to the questions. The borrower
feels it, and the call never recovers.

**What to do:**

1. **Stop.**
2. **Pause.** A real one.
3. **Acknowledge it, briefly and without performance.**
4. **Let them decide whether to continue.**

> **[pause]** *"I'm sorry. That's a lot to be dealing with."*
> **[pause — let it sit]**
> *"Do you want to keep going with this, or would another day be better?"*

**Offering to stop is what makes it genuine.** They almost always continue, and the
conversation is completely different afterward — because you did the one thing nobody on
a sales call does.

**Never** use hardship as leverage. Never say "so this would really help you then." The
moment you monetize their vulnerability, they feel it, and you deserve to lose the deal.

Paula in Call 2 said *"maybe I'll be dead by the time it's due."* It was laughed past.
That was a real statement about her age, her timeline, and her estate — and it was the
most important thing she said. Handled properly, it's the entire conversation about
whether a 30-year term is even appropriate for her.

## 8. The borrower who wants something you shouldn't sell them

Sometimes the deal works for you and doesn't work for them. Refinancing a 3% into a 7%
for a want rather than a need. Consolidating cards they'll immediately run back up.
Pulling equity to fund something you can tell is a bad idea.

**Say so.**

> *"I can do this. I want to be honest with you about whether you should. You'd be giving
> up a 3.1% on your whole balance to get $40,000, and over the life of the loan that
> costs you a lot more than $40,000. If this were my brother, I'd tell him to look at the
> second lien instead — or to wait. Do you want me to price that instead?"*

You lose some deals this way. You gain a reputation that generates referrals for a
decade. And you stay on the right side of UDAAP's "abusive" prong, which is aimed
precisely at taking advantage of a borrower's reliance on you to act in their interest
(`../4-COMPLIANCE/04-respa-ecoa-udaap.md`).

## The two rules that govern all hard cases

**1. Classify before you respond.** Is this an objection or a revocation? Venting or a
removal request? Unqualified today or unqualified forever? The wrong classification
produces the wrong response no matter how good your technique is.

**2. The honest answer is almost always the strong answer.** Every hard case above
resolves best with directness — "I can't beat that rate," "you should stay with them,"
"I can't do this today, here's what would change it," "I don't know, let me find out."

Being the only honest person on their phone is the most durable competitive advantage
available to you.

---

## What you must be able to do

- Classify hostility as venting vs. revocation, instantly
- Treat a gatekeeper as a decision-maker
- Walk away from a late-stage locked loan and still capture the relationship
- Tell an unqualified borrower the truth and give them a path
- Say "I don't know" and deliver an answer the same day
- Handle an emotional disclosure without exploiting it

**Self-check:** A borrower says *"I've got a 2.8%, I'm 71, my wife passed last year, and I
need $30,000 for medical bills."* What do you do?

> *Pause. "I'm sorry about your wife." Pause again. Then: do not refinance that 2.8%
> under any circumstance — it would be indefensible. Price a small fixed second lien and
> be honest that his existing mortgage stays untouched. Also ask, gently, whether he's
> looked at whether the medical bills can be negotiated or put on a payment plan first,
> because sometimes the cheapest loan is no loan. If a second is still the answer,
> structure the smallest one that solves the problem — not the largest one he qualifies
> for.*
