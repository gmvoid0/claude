# Income Calculation

The most technical part of your job, and the part where knowing more than your competitor
wins deals outright.

**The governing principle:** underwriting counts income that is **stable, verifiable, and
likely to continue**. Everything below follows from that.

---

## W-2 income

### Salaried — the easy case

```
Annual salary ÷ 12 = monthly qualifying income
```

**Documents:** 30 days of paystubs, 2 years of W-2s, verbal verification of employment at
closing.

### Hourly

```
Hourly rate × guaranteed weekly hours × 52 ÷ 12
```

If hours vary, a **2-year average** is used.

### Overtime, bonus, and commission

**Requires a 2-year history**, then averaged over 24 months.

```
(Year 1 + Year 2) ÷ 24 = monthly qualifying amount
```

**The declining-income rule:** if the current year is *lower* than the prior year,
underwriting generally uses the **lower, more conservative** figure — and may require an
explanation.

### Tyler in Call 3 — a real complication

> *"My base salary is $63,500, but after my monthly bonuses I'm around like $79,000 to
> $83,000. Obviously, this year's W-2 won't reflect that because I took 12 weeks off."*

He identified a genuine problem and nobody engaged with it.

**The issue:** his bonus income needs a 2-year average, and this year's W-2 will be
suppressed by 12 weeks of leave. A naive 2-year average will understate his real earning
power.

**What should have been said:**

> *"That's actually an important detail. Bonus income needs a two-year average, and your
> leave is going to drag this year's number down. There's a case to be made that
> underwriting should annualize around the leave — that's a documentable, non-recurring
> event. Can you get me a letter from HR confirming the leave dates and that your bonus
> structure is unchanged? That's the difference between qualifying on $63,500 and
> qualifying on $80,000."*

That's a **$17,000 swing in qualifying income**, and it required knowing one rule.

### Employment gaps

Gaps under 30 days generally aren't an issue. Longer gaps require explanation, and gaps
over six months may require re-establishing a 2-year history — though returning to the
same field usually helps.

**Tyler's 12-week paternity leave** is a protected, documentable leave and should be
handled as such, not as a job gap.

## Self-employed income

**The hardest and most valuable skill in income calculation.** Most LOs are bad at it,
which is exactly why being good at it wins files.

### The core problem

A business owner's tax return is engineered to **minimize taxable income**. Good tax
strategy, terrible mortgage qualification.

**Say this early to every self-employed borrower:**

> *"Here's the thing about self-employed files, and I'd rather tell you now than in three
> weeks. Underwriting doesn't look at what your business brings in — it looks at what you
> show after write-offs. Most good business owners write off aggressively, which makes you
> look poor on paper. So let's find out what your returns actually say before we plan
> around them."*

### Documents required

- **2 years personal tax returns**, all schedules
- **2 years business tax returns**, if the entity files separately
- **Year-to-date P&L**, sometimes CPA-prepared
- **K-1s** for partnerships and S-corps
- **Business license** or CPA letter confirming the business is active

### By entity type

| Entity | Form | Where the income is |
|---|---|---|
| Sole proprietor | Schedule C (1040) | Net profit, line 31 |
| Partnership | Form 1065 + K-1 | K-1 ordinary income + guaranteed payments |
| S-corporation | Form 1120S + K-1 | K-1 income + W-2 wages paid to owner |
| C-corporation | Form 1120 | W-2 wages; dividends only with history |
| LLC | Varies by election | Depends how it's taxed |

**Always ask the entity type.** It changes the entire calculation, and in Call 4 it was
never asked of a 22-year business owner.

### The add-backs — where you find income

Certain **non-cash** deductions get added back to qualifying income, because they reduced
taxable income without reducing cash flow:

| Add-back | Why |
|---|---|
| **Depreciation** | Non-cash paper deduction |
| **Depletion** | Non-cash |
| **Amortization** | Non-cash |
| **Casualty losses** | Typically non-recurring |
| **Business use of home** | Non-cash allocation |
| **One-time expenses** | If genuinely non-recurring and documented |

**Depreciation is the big one, especially in trucking.** Vehicles and equipment depreciate
heavily.

### Fasani, Call 4 — the missed analysis

A 22-year trucking business owner. Trucks are depreciating assets. His returns almost
certainly show **substantial depreciation** — which adds straight back to qualifying
income.

**Nobody asked about the entity type, the depreciation, or the deposits.** The call
collected "I used to make really good money" and moved on.

### The calculation, illustrated

Schedule C sole proprietor:

```
Net profit (line 31)          $48,000
+ Depreciation                 $22,000
+ Business use of home          $3,600
+ Depletion                         $0
= Adjusted annual income      $73,600
÷ 12                           $6,133/month qualifying
```

**That borrower "makes $48,000" on paper and qualifies on $73,600.** Knowing this is the
difference between a denial and an approval, and it's why self-employed borrowers should
always be worked by someone who understands returns.

### The 2-year rule and declining income

- Generally **2 years of self-employment** required
- Some programs allow 1 year with strong compensating factors and prior experience in the
  same field
- **If year 2 is lower than year 1, the lower figure is typically used**, and a
  declining-income explanation may be required

**"Business slowed down but it's coming back"** — Fasani's exact situation — is
specifically the profile underwriting scrutinizes hardest. It needed a real conversation
and got none.

### When the returns don't work

Go to **bank statement or P&L programs** (`product-5-non-qm.md`). The question that
routes it:

> *"Forget the tax returns for a second. In a normal month, how much comes into your
> business account? Just gross deposits — not profit, not after write-offs."*

## Other income types

### Rental income

```
Gross rent × 0.75 = qualifying income
```

The 25% haircut covers vacancy and maintenance. Documented from Schedule E, a lease, or
the appraiser's market rent analysis.

**If Schedule E shows a loss**, that loss counts *against* the borrower as a liability.

### Retirement, pension, Social Security

Documented from award letters, 1099-Rs, and statements. Must be shown to continue for at
least 3 years.

**Much of this income is non-taxable and can be grossed up** — see below.

### Disability

Documented from an award letter. Must show continuance.

**VA disability is tax-free and grosses up.** It also drives the VA funding fee exemption
(`product-3-va.md`).

### Alimony and child support

- Requires a **court order or written agreement**
- Requires typically **6 months of receipt history**
- Must continue at least **3 more years**

Under ECOA you may ask about it for qualifying purposes — but never in a way that
discourages. `../4-COMPLIANCE/04-respa-ecoa-udaap.md`

### Part-time and second jobs

Generally requires a **2-year history**.

### Assets as income

**Asset depletion / asset utilization** converts liquid assets into qualifying income
(`product-5-non-qm.md`). Best for retirees and post-liquidity-event borrowers.

## Grossing up non-taxable income

```
Qualifying income = Actual × 1.25
```

The 25% factor is common; it varies by lender and program.

**Applies to:** VA disability, much of Social Security, certain child support, some
pension and retirement income, certain municipal bond income.

**Michael's $148/month** → roughly $185 for qualifying. Small, but the principle is large:
on $2,500/month of tax-free income, grossing up adds over $600/month of qualifying power.

**Ask every borrower:** *"Is any of your income non-taxable?"* Most don't know it matters.

## The income interview

Run this on every call:

1. **"Are you W-2, 1099, or self-employed?"**
2. **If W-2:** *"Salary or hourly? Any overtime, bonus, or commission? How long have you
   been there?"*
3. **If self-employed:** *"How long have you owned it? What's the entity — sole prop, LLC,
   S-corp? Are your returns filed and current? In a normal month, how much is deposited
   into the business account?"*
4. **"Any other income? Rental, retirement, disability, child support, a side business?"*
5. **"Is any of that non-taxable?"**
6. **"Any gaps in employment in the last two years?"**

**Six questions. Two minutes.** They give you almost everything you need to know whether
this deal is real — and every one of them was available in the four transcripts.

## What you must be able to do

- Calculate W-2 income for salaried, hourly, and variable earners
- Explain the 2-year rule and the declining-income rule
- Identify entity type and locate income on each return type
- **Name the add-backs and calculate adjusted self-employed income**
- Pivot to deposits when returns won't work
- Gross up non-taxable income
- Run the six-question income interview

**Self-check:** Borrower owns an S-corp landscaping business, 6 years. K-1 shows $41,000
ordinary income, he pays himself $60,000 in W-2 wages, and the 1120S shows $28,000
depreciation. Roughly what's his qualifying income?

> *W-2 wages $60,000 + K-1 ordinary income $41,000 + depreciation add-back $28,000 =
> roughly $129,000 annually, about $10,750/month. He'd tell you he "makes $60,000."
> Knowing to add the K-1 and the depreciation more than doubles his qualifying income —
> and confirm both years to check the income isn't declining.*
