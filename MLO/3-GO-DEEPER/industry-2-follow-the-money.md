# Follow the Money

Where mortgage money comes from and what actually moves rates. This is the knowledge that
makes you sound like an expert in thirty seconds.

---

## The two markets

### The primary market
Where you work. Borrower ↔ lender. A loan is created.

### The secondary market
Where loans are **bought and sold** after they're made. Vastly larger, and it's what
actually sets your rates.

**Why it exists:** if lenders had to hold every loan for 30 years, they'd run out of money
after a few dozen loans. Selling loans recycles capital immediately, which is what makes
mass-market mortgage lending possible at all.

---

## The GSEs

**Fannie Mae** (FNMA) and **Freddie Mac** (FHLMC) are **Government-Sponsored
Enterprises**. They don't lend to consumers. They **buy loans from lenders**, guarantee
them, and package them into securities.

They have been in **federal conservatorship since 2008**.

**What they actually do:**

1. **Buy loans** that meet their standards
2. **Set the standards** — this is why "conventional guidelines" exist
3. **Guarantee** the securities against borrower default
4. **Securitize** — pool loans into MBS and sell to investors

**Why you care:** Fannie and Freddie's rulebooks *are* conventional underwriting. When
your AE says "DU won't approve that," they mean Fannie's automated system said no.

**Ginnie Mae** is different — it's an actual government agency that guarantees securities
backed by **FHA, VA, and USDA** loans.

### Conforming vs. non-conforming

A **conforming loan** meets GSE standards, including the loan limit.

**2026 conforming limits:**

| Units | Baseline | High-cost ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **$832,750** | **$1,249,125** |
| 2 | $1,066,250 | — |
| 3 | $1,288,800 | — |
| 4 | $1,601,750 | — |

Alaska, Hawaii, Guam, and the USVI use $1,249,125 as their baseline.

Above the limit is a **jumbo** loan — held on bank balance sheets or sold privately,
different guidelines, often stricter.

## Mortgage-backed securities

An **MBS** is a bond backed by a pool of mortgages. Investors buy the right to receive the
borrowers' payments.

**The chain:**

```
Your borrower's $1,331/month payment
    ↓ servicer collects, keeps a small fee
    ↓ passes the rest through
MBS investors — pension funds, insurers, banks, foreign central banks
```

**The critical implication: your borrower's rate is determined by what investors are
willing to pay for that bond.**

---

## What actually moves rates

### The mechanism

MBS are bonds. Bond prices and yields move **inversely**:

- Investors **buy** MBS → prices up → yields down → **mortgage rates fall**
- Investors **sell** MBS → prices down → yields up → **mortgage rates rise**

### What drives investor behavior

| Factor | Effect on rates |
|---|---|
| **Inflation** | Higher inflation → higher rates. Inflation erodes fixed payments, so investors demand more yield. **The single biggest driver.** |
| **10-year Treasury** | Mortgage rates track it closely, with a spread on top |
| **Fed policy** | Indirect. Sets short-term rates; influences expectations. |
| **Fed balance sheet** | When the Fed buys MBS directly, rates fall (this drove 2020–21) |
| **Employment data** | Strong jobs → inflation fear → rates up |
| **Flight to safety** | Crisis → investors buy bonds → rates fall |
| **Housing supply/demand** | Affects volume more than rate |

### The Fed misconception — worth mastering

**"The Fed cut rates, why didn't my mortgage rate drop?"**

You will hear this constantly. The answer:

> *"The Fed sets the federal funds rate — what banks charge each other overnight. Your
> mortgage rate comes from the mortgage bond market. They're related, but not the same.
> Sometimes a Fed cut actually pushes mortgage rates **up**, if investors read it as
> inflationary — and inflation is what mortgage investors fear most. It catches people
> out all the time."*

Being able to say that clearly puts you ahead of most loan officers.

## The spread

Mortgage rates sit **above** the 10-year Treasury by a **spread** — compensation for
prepayment risk, credit risk, and servicing costs.

Historically the spread has averaged roughly 1.7–2.0 percentage points, and it widens in
periods of volatility and uncertainty.

**Why you care:** when the spread widens, mortgage rates can rise even when Treasuries
don't. This is a genuine, non-obvious explanation for why rates "didn't follow the news."

## Where your price actually comes from

Walking backward from your borrower's rate:

```
1. MBS market sets base pricing for a given coupon
2. Lender adds margin, servicing value, and costs → RATE SHEET
3. LLPAs adjust for risk (FICO, LTV, occupancy, purpose, product)
4. Your compensation is added
5. = The rate you quote
```

### LLPAs

**Loan-Level Price Adjustments** are risk-based pricing add-ons set by the GSEs.

Priced by a grid crossing **FICO** against **LTV**, with further adjustments for:

- Occupancy — investment property costs more
- Purpose — cash-out costs more than rate-and-term
- Property type — condos, 2–4 units cost more
- Product — ARM vs fixed
- Loan amount — very small loans price worse
- Subordinate financing present

**Why this matters on the phone:** LLPAs are why "what's your rate?" is genuinely
unanswerable without knowing FICO, LTV, occupancy, and purpose. That's not a dodge — it's
mechanically true, and explaining it is more persuasive than deflecting.

> *"I can't give you a real number until I know four things, and it's not me being cagey —
> pricing is literally a grid. Your score and your equity position cross on that grid, and
> a 20-point score difference can move your rate a quarter point. I'd rather ask four
> questions than give you a number that changes."*

### Rate sheets

Each wholesale lender publishes a daily (sometimes intraday) rate sheet showing rate vs.
**price**:

| Rate | Price |
|---|---|
| 6.500 | 98.750 |
| 6.750 | 99.625 |
| 7.000 | **100.250** |
| 7.250 | 100.875 |

**Price above 100 = rebate** (lender credit available). **Below 100 = discount** (borrower
pays points).

This is the mechanism behind **"no closing cost" loans** — you take a higher rate, the
rebate covers the costs. It's not free; it's a trade, and being honest about the trade is
a selling point.

## Servicing

After closing, someone collects the payments. That's the **servicer**, and it may not be
the lender.

The right to service is itself an asset — **MSRs** (Mortgage Servicing Rights) — bought
and sold separately from the loans.

**Why you care:**

- Your borrower's loan often gets sold. **Tell them this on the sales call** — it's normal
  and it prevents an angry phone call in month two.
- The servicer's retention department will try to keep them when they refinance. It's why
  competitors call your past clients.
- **The servicer's incentive is retention. Yours should be advice.** That's a genuine
  competitive difference and worth explaining.

## The 2026 market

| | |
|---|---|
| 30-year fixed | **~6.8–7.0%** (Aug 2026) |
| Projected | 6.2–6.5% by year end (Fannie Mae / MBA) |
| Legacy rates outstanding | **2.5%–4%** for anyone who transacted 2020–22 |

### The lock-in effect

Tens of millions of homeowners hold rates far below market. Moving means giving up that
rate. So they don't move. This:

- Suppresses housing inventory
- Suppresses purchase volume
- **Kills rate-and-term refinance almost entirely**
- **Makes second liens the growth product** — the only way to access equity without
  surrendering the first-lien rate

**This single dynamic defines your entire market.** It's why
`product-6-second-liens-heloc.md` matters more than any other product document
in this system.

## What you must be able to do

- Explain the secondary market in three sentences
- Explain why the Fed cutting doesn't automatically lower mortgage rates
- Explain what an LLPA is and why it makes "what's your rate" unanswerable
- Read a rate sheet and explain rebate vs. discount
- Explain the lock-in effect and why it makes second liens the product of this market

**Self-check:** *"Why is your rate different from what I saw advertised?"*

> *"Advertised rates are the best-case scenario — top credit tier, low loan-to-value,
> primary residence, rate-and-term, and usually with points paid. Pricing is a grid: your
> score and equity cross on it, and cash-out prices higher than a straight refinance.
> None of that is a lender being sneaky, it's how the GSEs price risk. Tell me your rough
> score and what you're trying to do, and I'll tell you where you actually land."*
