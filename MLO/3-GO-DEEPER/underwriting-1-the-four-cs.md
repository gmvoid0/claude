# The Four Cs

Every underwriting decision, every document request, and every condition maps to one of
four questions. Learn this frame and underwriting stops feeling arbitrary.

---

## The frame

| C | The question | What's examined |
|---|---|---|
| **Credit** | Do they pay people back? | FICO, tradelines, derogatories, history |
| **Capacity** | Can they afford it? | Income, employment, DTI |
| **Capital** | Do they have money? | Down payment, reserves, source of funds |
| **Collateral** | Is the house worth it? | Appraisal, LTV, condition, marketability |

When a borrower asks *"why do you need this?"*, the answer is always one of these four.
Saying which one makes you sound like you understand the process — because you do.

---

## The compensating factor principle

**This is the most useful thing in this document.**

Underwriting is not a checklist of independent pass/fail tests. It's a **risk assessment
in aggregate**. Weakness in one C can be offset by strength in another.

| Weakness | Compensating strength |
|---|---|
| High DTI | Large reserves, long job tenure, high FICO, low LTV |
| Low FICO | Large down payment, low DTI, substantial reserves |
| Short job history | Same field, degree in the field, large reserves |
| Recent credit event | Documented one-time cause, strong recovery, low LTV |
| Thin credit file | Alternative tradelines (rent, utilities), large down payment |

**Why this matters on the phone:** when a borrower says "I got denied," don't accept the
denial as the whole story. Ask what the reason was — then look for the compensating
factor.

> *"They said your DTI was too high? Okay — how much do you have in savings and
> retirement after closing? Because reserves are a compensating factor, and some lenders
> weight them a lot more heavily than others. A no at one lender is one lender's model,
> not the market."*

**This is the single strongest argument for using a broker**, and you should make it
often.

# Credit

Full detail in `underwriting-2-credit.md`. The essentials:

### The score

- Range 300–850
- **Tri-merge** report pulls all three bureaus
- The **middle** score governs for one borrower
- For joint applicants, typically the **lower of the two middle scores**

### The tiers

| FICO | Position |
|---|---|
| 760+ | Best pricing available |
| 740–759 | Excellent |
| 720–739 | Very good |
| 700–719 | Good |
| 680–699 | Fine, pricing starts to cost |
| 660–679 | Acceptable |
| 640–659 | Limited options |
| 620–639 | Conventional floor |
| 580–619 | FHA territory |
| 500–579 | FHA at 10% down; very few lenders |

**Tiers move in 20-point bands.** A borrower at 698 who gets to 700 can meaningfully
improve pricing. This is worth checking — a small paydown on a credit card before the pull
can be worth real money.

### What's examined beyond the score

- Payment history, especially **mortgage** lates
- Number and age of tradelines
- Credit utilization
- Recent inquiries and new accounts
- Collections, charge-offs, judgments, liens
- Bankruptcies, foreclosures, short sales, and their seasoning

# Capacity

### Income documentation by type

| Borrower type | Documents |
|---|---|
| **W-2 salaried** | 30 days paystubs, 2 years W-2s, verbal VOE at closing |
| **W-2 hourly/variable** | Same, plus a 2-year average of variable income |
| **Commission/bonus** | 2-year history required; averaged |
| **Self-employed** | 2 years personal + business returns, YTD P&L, K-1s |
| **1099** | 2 years 1099s and returns |
| **Retired** | Award letters, 1099-R, statements |
| **Rental** | Schedule E, or leases |

Full detail in `underwriting-3-income.md`.

### The two-year rule

Most income types require a **2-year history** to count. This catches people constantly:

- Bonus and commission income under 2 years usually can't be used
- Self-employment under 2 years is usually excluded
- **A job gap** can require re-establishing history

**Tyler in Call 3** had a 12-week paternity leave gap. **Fasani in Call 4** has a business
that "slowed down" — meaning declining income, which underwriting treats far more harshly
than stable income.

**Declining self-employed income is a specific problem:** if year two is lower than year
one, most guidelines require using the *lower* figure, and some require an explanation of
why the business isn't continuing to decline.

# Capital

### What underwriting wants to know

1. **Do they have enough** for down payment and closing costs?
2. **Where did it come from?** — sourcing
3. **What's left afterward?** — reserves

### Sourcing and seasoning

**Any large deposit will be questioned.** "Large" is generally anything unusual relative to
their income pattern.

**Acceptable, with documentation:**
- Payroll deposits
- Sale of an asset (with bill of sale)
- Tax refunds
- **Gift funds** — requires a gift letter, proof of donor's ability, and a paper trail
- Retirement withdrawal (with statements)

**Problems:**
- **Cash deposits.** Undocumented cash is generally unusable. This catches self-employed
  and tipped borrowers constantly.
- Undocumented transfers between accounts
- Loans from family disguised as gifts

**Seasoning:** funds in the account for **60+ days** are generally considered seasoned and
don't require sourcing.

### The warning to give every borrower

> *"Between now and closing, don't move large amounts of money between accounts. If you
> do, keep every statement, because underwriting will ask where it came from — and 'I
> moved it from my other account' isn't an answer, they'll want to see both sides. And if
> anyone's giving you money for this, tell me now so we can paper it as a gift properly."*

### Reserves

Liquid assets remaining **after** closing, measured in months of PITI.

| Loan type | Typical reserve requirement |
|---|---|
| Conventional, primary | Often 0–2 months |
| Investment property | 6+ months |
| **Jumbo** | **6–12+ months** |
| Multiple financed properties | Escalating |

**Retirement accounts usually count at a discounted percentage** — confirm the treatment
with your AE.

**Reserves are the most powerful compensating factor available.** A borrower with 12
months of reserves gets latitude on DTI that a borrower with none never will.

# Collateral

### The appraisal

An independent opinion of value. **You may not influence it** — appraiser independence is
federal law (`industry-4-cast-of-characters.md`).

### What it determines

- **LTV** — the loan-to-value that drives pricing and eligibility
- **Condition** — habitability, safety, required repairs
- **Marketability** — could it be resold if foreclosed?

### When value comes in low

Options, in order of usefulness:

1. **Reconsideration of value (ROV)** — submit better comparable sales. Real process, low
   success rate.
2. **Increase the down payment** to preserve LTV
3. **Reduce the loan amount**
4. **Renegotiate the purchase price** (purchase only)
5. **Order a new appraisal with a different lender** — expensive, and the borrower pays
   again

**Set expectations before it happens:**

> *"One thing to know up front — the appraisal is the piece neither of us controls. It's an
> independent third party by law, and I'm legally not allowed to influence it. Most come in
> where expected. If it doesn't, here's what we'd do..."*

That thirty seconds prevents an angry phone call.

### Property types that complicate things

| Type | Issue |
|---|---|
| **Condos** | Project must be warrantable — HOA finances, owner-occupancy ratio, litigation. Non-warrantable needs specialty lending. |
| **Manufactured homes** | Limited lenders, must be affixed to a permanent foundation, HUD tags required |
| **Rural / large acreage** | Comparables are hard to find; excess land may not be valued |
| **Mixed-use** | Commercial portion creates problems |
| **Unique properties** | Log homes, earth homes, geodesic — marketability concerns |
| **Properties in poor condition** | FHA/VA especially strict |

**Ask about property type early.** Finding out at appraisal that it's a non-warrantable
condo is three wasted weeks.

## Reading the file like an underwriter

Before you submit, ask yourself these four questions. If you can't answer one, you have a
condition coming.

| C | The question |
|---|---|
| **Credit** | What's the worst thing on this credit report, and what's my explanation? |
| **Capacity** | Is this income stable, documentable, and likely to continue? |
| **Capital** | Where did every dollar come from, and what's left after closing? |
| **Collateral** | Will the appraisal support this, and is the property lendable? |

**Doing this before you submit is what separates LOs whose files close from those whose
files stall.** It's also what lets you have honest conversations on the phone instead of
surprises at week three.

## What you must be able to do

- Name the Four Cs and map any document request to one
- Explain the compensating factor principle
- Use it to reopen a borrower's prior denial
- Explain sourcing, seasoning, and why cash deposits are a problem
- Deliver the money-movement warning
- Explain appraiser independence and what happens when value comes in low

**Self-check:** Borrower has a 640 FICO, 48% DTI, $85,000 in savings after closing, and
has been at the same job 11 years. Conventional wants 45% DTI max. What do you do?

> *Don't accept the 45% as fixed. He has two strong compensating factors — roughly 18+
> months of reserves and 11 years of job tenure, both of which AUS weighs. Run it through
> DU; if it refers, run LPA, because they weight risk differently. If both refuse, ask
> your AE which lenders have the most flexible overlays at this profile. Also check whether
> paying off one small debt drops him under 45% — sometimes $180/month of payment is the
> whole problem. That's four moves before you'd tell him no.*
