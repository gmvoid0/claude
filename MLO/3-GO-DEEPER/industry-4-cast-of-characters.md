# Cast of Characters

Who does what, who can help you, and who can kill your deal.

---

## Your side

### Loan Officer (you)
Find borrowers, structure loans, take applications, select the lender, manage the
relationship. **You are the only person on this list whose job is the borrower's
experience.**

### Loan Officer Assistant (LOA)
Admin support — document chasing, scheduling, CRM. **Unlicensed LOAs may not take
applications, quote rates, or negotiate terms.** Know exactly where that line is; it's an
NMLS violation to cross it (`../4-COMPLIANCE/05-your-license.md`).

### Processor
Collects, organizes, and submits documents; clears conditions.

**Your relationship with your processor is the most operationally important one you
have.** A good processor saves deals. Treat them accordingly:
- Give them complete files, not fragments
- Never promise a borrower something without checking timing
- Tell them immediately when something changes

### Branch manager / broker owner
Runs the shop, sets comp plans, holds lender approvals, owns compliance.

---

## The lender's side

### Account Executive (AE)
Your rep at each wholesale lender. **The most underused relationship for a new LO.**

They tell you if a scenario will fly before you submit, escalate stuck files, get
exceptions considered, and warn you about guideline changes. Call them *before* you
submit anything unusual. `industry-3-channels-and-your-seat.md`

### Underwriter
Decides. Reviews the file against guidelines and issues approval with conditions, or
denial.

**How to work with them:**
- Never argue. Present facts and documentation.
- A **letter of explanation (LOE)** is your tool for anything unusual
- Anticipate conditions and address them upfront — a large deposit will *always* be
  questioned, so document the source before they ask
- If you think they're wrong on a guideline, escalate politely through your AE with the
  guideline citation, not with volume

### Closer / funder
Prepares final documents and wires funds. Errors here cause closing-day disasters.

### Servicer
Collects payments post-closing. Often not the original lender.

---

## Third parties

### Appraiser
Determines value. **Independent by law** — under appraiser independence rules you may
**not** attempt to influence value, suggest a target, or select the appraiser directly.

**What you may do:** provide factual information (recent comparable sales, a list of
improvements) through the proper channel. **What you may never do:** tell them what number
you need. That's a federal violation and it ends careers.

**When value comes in low:** you may request a **reconsideration of value (ROV)** with
documented comparable sales. It's a real process with a low success rate — set
expectations accordingly.

### Title company
Researches ownership, identifies liens and judgments, issues title insurance, often
handles closing.

**Title problems are a common surprise:** old liens, judgments, contractor liens,
easements, name mismatches, or a deceased co-owner. On a refinance, an unexpected lien
can kill a deal at week three. Ask early: *"Any liens or judgments we should know about?
Anything from a divorce?"*

### Escrow officer
Holds funds, coordinates signing, disburses at closing. In some states an attorney does
this instead.

### Real estate agent
On purchases, often the reason the borrower called you at all.

**Their incentive is the transaction closing on time.** Align with that: fast
pre-approvals, honest timelines, proactive updates. An agent who trusts you sends you
every buyer they meet. See `../5-SALES/17-referral-partners.md` — and note the RESPA
Section 8 limits on that relationship
(`../4-COMPLIANCE/04-respa-ecoa-udaap.md`).

### Insurance agent
Homeowners insurance is now a **major** obstacle in some markets. Premiums have risen
sharply, and in coastal and wildfire-exposed areas coverage can be difficult to obtain at
all.

**This is a live deal-killer.** A borrower who qualifies at a $1,200/year premium may not
qualify at $6,000. Ask about insurance early on any property in an exposed market — and
remember Paula in Call 2 was paying **$6,000/year**, which nobody flagged as unusual.

### CPA / financial planner
For self-employed borrowers the CPA is effectively a gatekeeper — they control when
returns get filed and how income is presented. Both are also excellent referral sources.

## Who can kill your deal

Know these so you can get ahead of them.

| Who | How | Prevent it by |
|---|---|---|
| **The borrower** | Opening new credit, changing jobs, moving money, missing a payment | Telling them explicitly not to, at application, in writing |
| **The appraiser** | Value comes in low | Setting expectations; providing comps early through proper channels |
| **The underwriter** | Conditions you didn't anticipate | Structuring the file correctly upfront |
| **Title** | Undisclosed liens or judgments | Asking about them on the sales call |
| **The insurance market** | Uninsurable or unaffordable | Asking early in exposed markets |
| **The competitor's retention desk** | Calling your borrower to keep them | Staying in contact; being the one who answers |
| **You** | Overpromising | Never promising a rate before pricing |

### The borrower-behavior warning

Give this verbatim at application. It prevents more dead deals than anything else you'll
do:

> *"Between now and closing, four rules. Don't open any new credit — no car, no
> furniture, no store card, not even a 'no interest for a year' offer. Don't close
> existing accounts either. Don't change jobs without calling me first. And don't move
> large amounts of money between accounts — if you do, keep every statement, because
> underwriting will ask where it came from. Any of those can blow this up at week three,
> and I'd rather annoy you now than lose your house later."*

## What you must be able to do

- Name every role and what they do
- Know exactly what an unlicensed LOA may not do
- Explain appraiser independence and what you may and may not provide
- Identify the seven deal-killers and how to prevent each
- Deliver the borrower-behavior warning from memory

**Self-check:** Your borrower calls at week two, excited — they just financed a new truck
because "the payment is low." What now?

> *Get the details immediately: amount financed, monthly payment, and whether it's already
> funded. That new payment goes into DTI and the inquiry hits their credit; both can
> break the approval. Recalculate DTI, then call your AE before you call the borrower back
> so you have a real answer. If it breaks the file, options are unwinding the purchase,
> restructuring the loan amount, or paying something else off. And note the lesson — this
> is the failure mode the borrower-behavior warning exists to prevent.*
