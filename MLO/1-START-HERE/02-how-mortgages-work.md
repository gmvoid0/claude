# Unit 1 — The Machine

What a mortgage actually is, where the money really comes from, and why the rules exist.
This is the foundation everything else sits on. UNDERSTAND-level unit — read it until
you can explain each section out loud.

---

## 1. A mortgage is two pieces of paper

When someone "gets a mortgage," they sign two key documents:

1. **The note** — an IOU. "I borrowed $300,000 and I promise to pay it back at 6.875%
   over 30 years in monthly payments."
2. **The security instrument** (called a *mortgage* or *deed of trust* depending on the
   state) — the collateral agreement. "If I break my promise, you can take the house
   and sell it to get your money back."

The note is the debt. The mortgage is the lender's claim on the house. People say
"mortgage" for the whole package, and so will we — but knowing the split explains a lot
later (why liens matter, why foreclosure works the way it does, why a "second" is riskier
than a "first").

**Say it like this:** *the note is the promise, the mortgage is the insurance on the
promise.*

## 2. The lender is not who you think

Here is the single most important fact in this whole unit:

> **The company that funds a loan almost never keeps it.**

If lenders kept every 30-year loan, they'd run out of money after a few dozen loans and
lending would stop. Instead, the industry works like a conveyor belt:

```
1. Lender wires money to close your borrower's loan
2. Days or weeks later, the lender SELLS that loan
3. The buyer pools it with thousands of similar loans
4. The pool becomes a BOND (a "mortgage-backed security" — MBS)
5. Investors buy the bond: pension funds, insurance companies, banks
6. Your borrower's monthly payment flows through to those investors
7. The lender's money is back — and gets lent again next week
```

The lender earns fees for making the loan and often keeps the **servicing** (the right
to collect payments for a fee). The actual 30-year risk lives with bond investors.

**Why you care, deeply:**

- **Rates come from the bond market**, not from any lender's generosity. Every lender
  is selling into the same market, which is why everyone's rates cluster together.
- **Guidelines exist because loans must be sellable.** A loan that doesn't follow the
  buyer's rulebook is a loan nobody will buy — so nobody will make it. When an
  underwriter says no, they're really saying "the buyer of this loan would reject it."
- **The Fed doesn't set mortgage rates.** It sets a short-term bank rate. Mortgage
  rates track what bond investors will pay — mostly driven by **inflation expectations**
  and the **10-year Treasury**. Sometimes a Fed cut sends mortgage rates *up*.

## 3. The two big buyers — Fannie and Freddie

**Fannie Mae** and **Freddie Mac** are giant government-sponsored companies whose whole
job is buying loans from lenders and turning them into bonds. Their rulebooks — who
qualifies, what documents are needed, what limits apply — ARE what the industry calls
**"conventional" guidelines**.

- A loan that follows their rules and fits under their size limit is **conforming**.
- 2026 size limit: **$832,750** for a single-family home in most counties
  (**$1,249,125** in designated high-cost counties).
- Bigger than the limit = **jumbo** — sold to private investors with their own,
  usually stricter, rules.

**Ginnie Mae** is a third name you'll hear: an actual government agency that guarantees
bonds made of **FHA, VA, and USDA** loans (the government-backed products in Unit 6).

## 4. The two questions every loan must answer

Every rule, document, and calculation in this entire guide exists to answer one of two
questions:

> **Question 1: Will this borrower pay it back?**
> (credit, income, assets — Unit 7)
>
> **Question 2: If they don't, is the house worth enough to make the investor whole?**
> (appraisal, LTV — Units 3 and 7)

When you're ever confused about *why* some requirement exists, trace it to one of these
two questions. It always fits.

## 5. The cast — who touches a loan

| Person | One-line job | Works for |
|---|---|---|
| **MLO (you)** | Structure the loan, take the application, pick the lender | The borrower's interests, at your brokerage |
| **Processor** | Collect and organize every document; feed underwriting | Your shop |
| **Underwriter** | Decide. Approve with conditions, or deny. | The lender |
| **Account Executive (AE)** | The lender's rep who helps YOU — scenario questions, stuck files | The lender |
| **Appraiser** | Independent opinion of the home's value | Nobody — independent by law |
| **Title company** | Verify ownership, find hidden claims, insure it | The transaction |
| **Escrow/closing agent** | Hold the money, run the signing, disburse | The transaction |
| **Closer/funder** | Prepare final docs, wire the money | The lender |
| **Servicer** | Collect payments after closing (often not the original lender) | The investor |

**Your seat — wholesale broker:** you don't lend money. You take the borrower's file and
shop it to the ~50 lenders your brokerage is approved with, pick the best fit, and manage
the file to closing. The lender underwrites and funds. Full picture of the file's journey
is Unit 8.

## 6. Amortization — why the early years feel like nothing

The payment is fixed. What's *inside* the payment changes every month.

$200,000 at 7% for 30 years → payment ≈ **$1,331/month**, every month. But:

| Point in time | Of that $1,331... interest | ...principal |
|---|---|---|
| Month 1 | ~$1,167 | ~$164 |
| Year 10 | ~$1,000 | ~$331 |
| Year 25 | ~$400 | ~$931 |

Early on, almost the whole payment is interest — because interest is charged on the
*remaining balance*, and the balance is biggest at the start. As the balance shrinks,
less of the payment goes to interest and more chews through principal. The crossover
accelerates.

**Three consequences to lock in:**

1. **A refinance resets the clock.** Someone 10 years into a loan who refinances into a
   fresh 30-year goes back to the interest-heavy start.
2. **Extra principal early is worth far more than extra principal late.**
3. **Total interest over 30 years is enormous** — on that $200k loan, about $279,000.

## 7. Escrow — the payment inside the payment

Most borrowers pay more than principal + interest each month. The servicer collects
extra for **property taxes** and **homeowners insurance**, holds it in an **escrow
account** (also called an *impound* account), and pays those bills when due.

So the real monthly payment is **PITI**: **P**rincipal + **I**nterest + **T**axes +
**I**nsurance (+ mortgage insurance and HOA dues where they apply).

**Why escrow payments change even on a "fixed" loan:** the P&I never moves, but taxes
and insurance go up. When they do, the servicer recalculates and the total payment
rises. This is the #1 reason a borrower with a fixed-rate loan says "my payment went
up" — the transcripts in the curriculum have a live example (a borrower whose escrow
rose when his insurance did).
