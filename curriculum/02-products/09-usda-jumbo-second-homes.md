# USDA, Jumbo, and Second Homes

Three products that come up less often but win deals when you know them.

---

# USDA

The most overlooked zero-down loan in America.

## What it is

The **USDA Guaranteed Rural Housing** loan — government-backed, **zero down payment**, for
properties in eligible areas.

| | |
|---|---|
| Down payment | **$0** |
| Typical FICO | 640 (some lenders lower with manual underwriting) |
| Occupancy | **Primary residence only** |
| Mortgage insurance | An upfront guarantee fee plus a smaller annual fee — **cheaper than FHA MIP** |
| Property | Must be in a USDA-eligible area |
| Income | Must be **under** the area limit |

## The two eligibility tests

Both must pass. Check them **before** you do anything else.

### 1. Property eligibility

"Rural" is far broader than people assume. Many outer-ring suburbs qualify. Whole towns
of 20,000+ people are frequently eligible.

**Check the address on USDA's eligibility map before you rule it out.** This is a
30-second lookup that most LOs never perform, which is precisely why the product is
underused.

### 2. Income eligibility

USDA has **maximum** income limits by county and household size — it's designed for
low-to-moderate income households. Household income includes non-borrower adult household
members, which surprises people.

## Why it's underused

Most LOs never check. They hear "rural" and assume farmland. Meanwhile a borrower with
zero down payment and a 660 score in an eligible suburb is getting steered into FHA with
3.5% down and permanent MIP.

**The prospecting angle:**

> *"Before we talk about the down payment — what's the address of the area you're looking
> in? There's a zero-down program most people have never heard of, and the eligible areas
> are much bigger than the name suggests. Takes me thirty seconds to check."*

That question, asked routinely, will find you deals other LOs walk past.

`[Verify current guarantee fee percentages, income limits, and property eligibility at
USDA's site — all are periodically revised.]`

# Jumbo

## What it is

Any loan **above the conforming limit** — over **$832,750** in most counties, or above the
high-cost ceiling of **$1,249,125** where that applies.

Jumbos can't be sold to Fannie or Freddie. They're held on bank balance sheets or sold
into private securitizations, which means **guidelines are set by whoever's buying** —
and they vary far more than conventional.

## Typical parameters

| | Typical |
|---|---|
| FICO | 700+, frequently 720–740+ |
| Down payment | 10–20%+ |
| DTI | Often capped around 43% |
| **Reserves** | **The distinguishing requirement** — often 6–12+ months of PITI |
| Documentation | Full, and thorough |
| Appraisals | Sometimes two required on very large loans |

> `[Jumbo guidelines vary enormously by investor. Verify per lender, per program.]`

## Reserves — the thing that kills jumbo deals

**Reserves** = liquid assets remaining *after* down payment and closing costs, measured in
months of PITI.

This is where jumbo files die. A borrower with strong income and a good score but no
post-closing liquidity fails, even when DTI looks fine.

**Ask early:**

> *"After the down payment and closing costs, how much would you have left in liquid
> savings and retirement? Jumbo lenders want to see a real cushion — often six months to a
> year of payments — and it's the thing that catches people out."*

Retirement accounts typically count at a discounted percentage. Confirm the treatment with
your AE.

## The broker advantage in jumbo

Jumbo guidelines vary **wildly** between investors. One lender wants 12 months of reserves;
another wants 6. One caps DTI at 40%; another allows 45% with compensating factors.

**A bank LO has one jumbo program. You have many.** This is where the broker channel's
advantage is most pronounced, and it's worth saying plainly:

> *"Jumbo is where having fifty lenders matters most. Conventional guidelines are the same
> everywhere — Fannie's rulebook is Fannie's rulebook. Jumbo is different. Every investor
> writes their own rules, and the spread between the strictest and the most flexible is
> enormous. A no at your bank means nothing about the rest of the market."*

## Non-QM jumbo

For high-net-worth borrowers who can't document income traditionally, the bank statement
and asset depletion programs in `10-non-qm.md` extend into jumbo territory. Business owners
buying $1.5M homes are a real and lucrative segment.

# Second homes and investment property

## The occupancy hierarchy

| Occupancy | Down | Pricing | Rental income counts? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary residence** | 3–5% | Best | N/A |
| **Second home** | 10%+ | Moderate LLPA | ❌ No |
| **Investment** | 15–25% | Significant LLPA | ✅ Yes, with a vacancy haircut |

## Second home requirements

To qualify as a second home rather than investment:

- Borrower must occupy it for **some portion of the year**
- Must be **suitable for year-round occupancy**
- Generally must be a **reasonable distance** from the primary residence
- Must **not** be subject to a rental or management agreement

That last one matters more than it used to. Short-term rental activity can reclassify a
"second home" as investment property, with materially worse pricing.

## Investment property

**Rental income counts toward qualifying** — typically at 75% of gross rent, with the 25%
haircut covering vacancy and maintenance.

Documented from a lease, or from the appraiser's market rent analysis.

**For serious investors, DSCR loans are usually better** than conventional — no personal
income verification, no DTI ceiling, and the property qualifies itself. `10-non-qm.md`

## The occupancy fraud warning

Borrowers **will** ask you to call an investment property a primary residence. The pricing
difference is large enough to tempt people.

**The answer is always no.**

> *"I can't, and I want to be clear about why. That's occupancy fraud — it's a federal
> crime, it's on a document you sign under penalty of perjury, and it would cost me my
> license. Let's do it correctly. The pricing difference is real, but you'd be risking
> criminal exposure to save a quarter point, and that's not a trade I'd let you make."*

Say it once, plainly, without moralizing, and move on to structuring it correctly.

## What you must be able to do

- Check USDA property and income eligibility before ruling it out
- Explain why USDA beats FHA for an eligible zero-down borrower
- State the 2026 jumbo threshold
- Ask about reserves early on any jumbo
- Explain why the broker advantage is largest in jumbo
- Distinguish second home from investment, and price accordingly
- Refuse occupancy misrepresentation cleanly

**Self-check:** Borrower has a 680 FICO, no down payment, $68,000 household income, buying
in a town of 15,000 about 40 minutes outside a metro area. What do you check first?

> *USDA eligibility, both tests. That town is very likely in an eligible area — "rural"
> covers far more than people assume — and $68,000 is likely under the county income limit
> depending on household size. If both pass, USDA gives zero down with cheaper mortgage
> insurance than FHA. If the property fails, FHA at 3.5% down is the fallback. Check the
> map before you do anything else; it takes thirty seconds and it's the difference between
> zero down and needing $10,000 they don't have.*
