# ARMs, Buydowns, and Points

Ways to change the rate. All three are underused, and one of them was the right answer for
a borrower in the transcripts.

---

# ARMs — Adjustable Rate Mortgages

## What they are

A rate fixed for an initial period, then adjusting periodically.

**The notation: 5/6, 7/6, 10/6**
- First number = **years** the initial rate is fixed
- Second number = **months** between adjustments after that

So a **7/6 ARM** is fixed for 7 years, then adjusts every 6 months.

## How the adjustment works

```
New rate = Index + Margin  (subject to caps)
```

- **Index** — a published market rate. Most current ARMs use **SOFR** (Secured Overnight
  Financing Rate).
- **Margin** — a fixed number added to the index. Set at origination, never changes.
  **This is the number to compare between lenders** — it determines the rate for the life
  of the loan after the fixed period.

## Caps — the borrower protection

Expressed as three numbers, e.g. **2/1/5**:

| Cap | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Initial** (2) | Max change at the first adjustment |
| **Periodic** (1) | Max change at each subsequent adjustment |
| **Lifetime** (5) | Max total increase over the starting rate |

**Always quote the worst case.** A borrower who understands the ceiling and accepts it is
a borrower who won't be angry in year eight.

> *"Your rate is 6.25% fixed for seven years. After that it can adjust every six months.
> Worst case, absolute maximum it could ever reach is 11.25%. I don't expect that, but you
> should decide knowing the ceiling, not the hope."*

## When an ARM is genuinely right

**ARMs price below fixed-rate loans** because the lender isn't carrying 30 years of rate
risk. That discount is real money for the right borrower.

| Situation | ARM appropriate? |
|---|---|
| Selling or moving within the fixed period | ✅ **Strongly** |
| Expecting to refinance when rates drop | ✅ Often |
| Income expected to rise substantially | ✅ Sometimes |
| Jumbo borrower with large reserves | ✅ Often |
| Fixed income, staying 30 years | ❌ No |
| Anxious about payment changes | ❌ No |
| Barely qualifying at the initial rate | ❌ **Absolutely not** |

## Tyler in Call 3 — the miss

> *"I'm planning on selling this house in the next two years... that 7.3 doesn't really
> scare me because I'm not going to ride out the entirety of the loan."*

**He described the textbook ARM borrower and was sold a 30-year fixed.**

A 5/6 ARM would price meaningfully below the 30-year fixed, *precisely because* he isn't
keeping it 30 years. He'd be paying for rate insurance he explicitly said he doesn't need.

**What should have been said:**

> *"You just told me you're selling in two years — that changes my recommendation
> completely. Has anyone shown you an ARM? A five-year ARM prices below a 30-year fixed
> exactly because you're not keeping it thirty years. You'd be paying extra for protection
> you'll never use. Let me price both."*

**The rule: when a borrower tells you their time horizon, match the product to it.**

## The ARM reputation problem

ARMs got a deserved bad name in 2008 — but those were different instruments: negative
amortization, payment-option ARMs, teaser rates that reset brutally, and no ability-to-repay
requirement.

**Today's ARMs are underwritten to the fully-indexed rate or the maximum first-adjustment
rate**, under ATR rules. A borrower must qualify at the higher rate, not the teaser.

Address the reputation directly:

> *"I know ARMs have a bad name from 2008. Those were a different product — teaser rates,
> negative amortization, and people qualified at a payment they could never actually
> afford. That's illegal now. You have to qualify at the higher rate, not the starting
> rate. This is a genuinely different instrument."*

# Temporary buydowns

## What they are

A **prepaid** reduction in the interest rate for the first years of the loan, funded by an
upfront lump sum held in escrow.

| Type | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 | Then |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **2-1** | −2% | −1% | Note rate | Note rate |
| **3-2-1** | −3% | −2% | −1% | Note rate |
| **1-0** | −1% | Note rate | — | — |

**Critically: the note rate never changes.** The borrower's actual loan is at the note
rate the whole time. The buydown fund subsidizes the difference each month.

## Who pays

Usually the **seller** or the **builder**, as a concession. Sometimes the lender. The
borrower can pay, but it rarely makes sense — they'd usually do better buying the rate
down permanently.

## Why it's powerful right now

In a market where sellers need to move houses, a buydown is often **cheaper for the
seller than a price reduction** and **worth more to the buyer**.

> *"Instead of asking the seller to drop the price $15,000, ask for a $15,000 buydown.
> Same cost to them. But a price cut saves you maybe $90 a month — the buydown saves you
> $400 a month for the first two years, right when money's tightest after moving in."*

**This is a genuinely valuable thing to teach real estate agents**, and it's how you become
the lender they call. `../09-business/49-referral-partners.md`

## The honest caveat

**Qualification is at the note rate**, not the bought-down rate. The borrower must be able
to afford the full payment when the subsidy ends.

Say it plainly:

> *"Two things you need to understand. One, your payment goes up in year three — it's
> scheduled, not a surprise, and I'll show you the exact numbers. Two, you have to qualify
> at the full rate anyway, so we're not stretching you into something you can't afford.
> And if rates come down before the buydown expires, we refinance and the unused buydown
> money typically gets credited back to you."*

That last point is a genuine benefit most borrowers don't know about.

# Points — permanent buydowns

## What they are

**Discount points** permanently lower the rate. **1 point = 1% of the loan amount.**

Typical: paying 1 point buys roughly 0.25% off the rate — but **this varies daily with
the rate sheet**, and sometimes points are a poor value. Always check the actual sheet.

## The break-even calculation

**Memorize this.**

```
Break-even (months) = Cost of points ÷ Monthly savings
```

### Example

$300,000 loan. 1 point = $3,000. Saves 0.25%, dropping the payment ~$50/month.

```
$3,000 ÷ $50 = 60 months = 5 years
```

**If they stay longer than 5 years, points win. Shorter, they lose.**

## The conversation

> *"You can buy the rate down — a point costs you $3,000 up front and saves about $50 a
> month. Break-even is five years. So the real question isn't whether points are good, it's
> how long you're keeping this loan. Honestly, in this market, most people shouldn't buy
> points — rates are projected to come down and if you refinance in eighteen months you've
> thrown that $3,000 away. What's your best guess on how long you're staying?"*

**Advising against points when they don't fit is one of the highest-trust moves available
to you**, and it costs you nothing — your compensation doesn't change either way under the
LO Comp Rule (`../01-industry/05-how-you-get-paid.md`).

## Negative points — lender credits

The reverse. Take a **higher** rate, receive a **credit** toward closing costs.

**This is the "no closing cost loan."**

| Situation | Best choice |
|---|---|
| Staying 7+ years, has cash | **Pay points** |
| Staying 2–3 years | **Take the credit** |
| Short on cash to close | **Take the credit** |
| Expects to refinance soon | **Take the credit** |

**Tyler in Call 3 should have been offered lender credits.** Selling in two years means he
should pay nothing up front and take a higher rate — he'll never recover an upfront cost.
It was never mentioned.

## The decision framework

Everything on this page reduces to one question:

> **How long is this borrower keeping this loan?**

| Horizon | Product | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Under 3 years** | ARM | Lender credits, no points |
| **3–7 years** | ARM or fixed | Run the break-even |
| **7+ years** | Fixed | Points may make sense |
| **Unknown** | Fixed | Neutral costs |

**Ask the question. Every time.**

> *"How long do you realistically see yourself in this house?"*

That single question determines product, term, and cost structure — and almost nobody
asks it.

---

## What you must be able to do

- Explain ARM notation, index, margin, and caps
- Quote the worst-case ARM rate honestly
- Identify the borrower whose horizon calls for an ARM
- Explain a 2-1 buydown, including who pays and the qualification rule
- Calculate points break-even in your head
- Recommend lender credits for a short-horizon borrower
- Ask the time-horizon question on every call

**Self-check:** Borrower is buying a $400,000 home, putting 20% down, transferring cities
in about 3 years for work. Rates: 30-yr fixed 6.875%, 5/6 ARM 6.25%. What do you
recommend?

> *The ARM, and take lender credits rather than paying points. He's out in three years —
> well inside the five-year fixed period, so he never sees an adjustment. The 0.625%
> saving on a $320,000 loan is roughly $130/month, about $4,700 over three years. Points
> would never break even. Show him the worst-case adjusted rate anyway so he's deciding
> with full information, and confirm the plan is firm — if the transfer might not happen,
> the calculus changes.*
