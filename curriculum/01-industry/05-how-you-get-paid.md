# How You Get Paid

Most new LOs don't understand their own compensation. That's a problem — you can't
negotiate what you can't calculate, and you can't explain it to a borrower who asks.

---

## Basis points

Compensation is quoted in **basis points (bps)**.

**1 bps = 0.01%. 100 bps = 1%.**

| bps | % of loan | On $250,000 |
|---|---|---|
| 100 | 1.00% | $2,500 |
| 125 | 1.25% | $3,125 |
| 150 | 1.50% | $3,750 |
| 200 | 2.00% | $5,000 |
| 275 | 2.75% | $6,875 |

**Your comp is a percentage of the loan amount** — not of the rate, not of the fees.

### The implication most new LOs miss

A $600,000 loan pays four times what a $150,000 loan pays, for roughly the same amount of
work. Often *less* work, because larger-loan borrowers tend to have cleaner files.

This should influence which leads you prioritize (`../06-cold-calling/35-aged-lead-playbook.md`)
— **not** which product you recommend. Steering by loan size is still steering.

---

## The LO Compensation Rule

Federal law (Reg Z, 12 CFR 1026.36(d)) governs how you can be paid. Enacted after 2008,
when LOs were routinely paid more for putting borrowers into worse loans.

### The four rules

**1. Comp may not vary based on loan terms.**
Not by rate, not by product, not by whether it's FHA or conventional. The **only**
permitted variable is loan amount.

**2. No dual compensation.**
On a single transaction you're paid by **either** the lender **or** the borrower. Never
both.

**3. No steering.**
You may not direct a borrower to a loan that pays you more.

**4. You generally may not reduce your own comp** to cover a pricing concession, outside
narrowly defined circumstances.

### Why this is genuinely good for you

It removes the incentive to steer, which means you can say this and mean it:

> *"I'm paid the same percentage whether you take a 30-year fixed or a 15, FHA or
> conventional, 6.5% or 7%. That's federal law — it exists specifically so I can't push
> you toward something that pays me better. So when I recommend something, it's because I
> think it's right, not because of what it pays."*

**Most borrowers have never heard this.** It's one of the most trust-generating things you
can say on a call, and it's completely true.

## Borrower-paid vs. lender-paid

As a broker, every loan is one or the other.

### Lender-paid compensation (LPC)

The lender pays you out of the rebate built into the rate.

- Your comp is set at a **fixed bps by your company**, across all loans
- The borrower pays no origination fee to you
- The cost is embedded in the rate

### Borrower-paid compensation (BPC)

The borrower pays you directly, from closing costs or proceeds.

- Comp can vary **by transaction** (but still not by loan terms)
- Allows a lower rate, since no rebate is needed to pay you
- Appears as an origination charge on the Loan Estimate

### Which is better?

It depends entirely on how long they're keeping the loan.

| Situation | Usually better |
|---|---|
| Staying long-term | **BPC** — lower rate, pays off over time |
| Selling or refinancing in 2–3 years | **LPC** — avoid upfront cost |
| Tight on cash to close | **LPC** |
| Cash-out with plenty of proceeds | **BPC** often works |

**Tyler in Call 3 is the textbook LPC case** — selling in two years, so paying upfront for
a lower rate is money he'll never recover. Nobody offered it.

**Explaining it to a borrower:**

> *"Two ways to handle my compensation. Either you pay it at closing and take a lower
> rate, or the lender pays it and you take a slightly higher rate but write no check. If
> you're staying ten years, paying it up front usually wins. If you're moving in two,
> it's the opposite. How long do you think you'll be in this house?"*

That question is genuinely consultative *and* it surfaces the time horizon you need for
product selection anyway.

## Reading a rate sheet

The mechanism behind all of this.

| Rate | Price |
|---|---|
| 6.500 | 98.750 |
| 6.750 | 99.625 |
| **7.000** | **100.250** |
| 7.250 | 100.875 |
| 7.500 | 101.500 |

**Price is a percentage of the loan amount.**

- **100.000 = par.** No points, no rebate.
- **Above 100 = rebate.** At 101.500 on a $250k loan, there's $3,750 available — to pay
  your comp, cover closing costs, or both.
- **Below 100 = discount.** At 98.750, the borrower pays 1.25 points ($3,125) to get that
  rate.

**How LPC works mechanically:** if your company's comp is 200 bps, you need 2.00 in
rebate. On this sheet, 7.500 gives 1.500 — not enough. You'd need to go higher, or the
borrower pays the difference.

**This is why "what's your rate" is unanswerable in the abstract.** The rate depends on
LLPAs, comp structure, and how costs are being covered.

### "No closing cost" loans

Not free. You take a higher rate; the rebate covers the costs.

**Be honest about the trade:**

> *"I can do this with no closing costs. It's not free — I take a rate about a quarter
> point higher and the lender credit covers your costs. Costs you about $40 a month, saves
> you about $4,000 today. If you're keeping this seven-plus years, pay the costs. If not,
> take the credit. Which is closer to your plan?"*

That last row is roughly what Amir Syed describes at his peak — 30+ loans a month at 40
hours a week. It's real, and it's built on a database and referral flow, not on dialing.

### What actually comes out

Before you plan your life around the gross:

- **Splits** — your company takes a share
- **Fees** — some shops charge per-file or per-month fees
- **Self-employment tax** — if you're 1099, roughly 15.3% on top of income tax
- **No withholding** — set aside 30–40% yourself, quarterly
- **No benefits** typically — health insurance, retirement are on you
- **Lead costs** — sometimes deducted

**A 150 bps gross is not 150 bps net.** Find out your real number in week one.

### The timing problem

**Mortgages take 30–45 days to close, and you're paid after funding.**

| Month | Activity | Income |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Dialing, first applications | **$0** |
| 2 | Applications, first locks | Little |
| 3 | First fundings | **Income arrives** |

**Most LOs who quit, quit in month two** — right before the thing they built starts
paying. Know this going in and budget for 90 days of nothing.

## Working backward from an income goal

This is the calculation that turns a wish into a plan.

**Goal: $150,000/year.** At $3,750 per file:

```
$150,000 ÷ $3,750         = 40 files/year
40 ÷ 12                   ≈ 3.3 fundings/month
3.3 ÷ 0.55 pull-through   ≈ 6 applications/month
6 ÷ 0.15 app rate         ≈ 40 conversations/month
40 ÷ 0.35                 ≈ 114 contacts/month
114 ÷ 0.12 contact rate   ≈ 950 dials/month
950 ÷ 21 days             ≈ 45 dials/day
```

**45 dials a day.** That's the whole plan. Not "work hard" — 45 dials.

## Questions to ask your manager this week

If you don't know these answers, you don't know your job:

1. What's my comp in bps? Is it tiered by volume?
2. LPC, BPC, or both? Who decides per file?
3. Is there a per-file fee or a monthly desk fee?
4. W-2 or 1099?
5. Do I pay for leads? At what cost?
6. When exactly am I paid after funding?
7. What's the branch's average loan amount?
8. What's a realistic ramp — when do new LOs here hit consistent production?
9. Are there minimums? What happens if I miss them?
10. Whose CRM is it, and what can I take with me?

**Number 10 matters more than it looks.** Your database is your career asset
(`../04-compliance/23-your-license-and-career.md`). Know now whether you're building it
for yourself or for someone else.

## What you must be able to do

- Convert bps to dollars instantly
- Explain the LO Comp Rule and why it's good for the borrower
- Explain BPC vs. LPC and match it to a borrower's time horizon
- Read a rate sheet and identify rebate vs. discount
- Explain a "no closing cost" loan honestly
- Work backward from an income goal to a daily dial count

**Self-check:** *"How much are you making on my loan?"*

> *"Fair question and I'll answer it straight. I'm paid a percentage of the loan amount —
> it's the same percentage regardless of what rate or product you pick, which is federal
> law. On a $250,000 loan that's about $3,750, and it's disclosed in writing on your Loan
> Estimate before you commit to anything. You'll see it in black and white. Any other
> questions about it?"*
>
> *Never dodge this. The straight answer is worth more than the fee.*
