# Teardown 3 — Tyler (581 FICO, forbearance, competitor mid-process)

**The call:** ~10 minutes. The longest and most technically detailed of the four.

**Surface verdict:** strong competitive takeaway. Borrower engaged, gave everything,
agreed to a callback.

**Actual verdict:** the best discovery in the set — and it contains the most legally
exposed sentence, plus the clearest case of a borrower stating his real need and being
ignored.

## What went right

This is the strongest call of the four on several dimensions.

### The diagnostic question
> *"What was the best rate they were able to offer you, Tyler?"*

Immediately after the brush-off. Excellent — it converts "I'm not interested" into a
data-gathering opportunity and gives him something to work with.

### Genuine curiosity about the competitor's number
> *"Wow, 7.2? ... Is there a reason why they're offering such a high rate, or what's
> going on there?"*

Good. It gets Tyler to explain his own situation (581 FICO, forbearance) without being
asked directly, which is much less confrontational than interrogating him about credit.

### The structure reconstruction — the best moment in all four transcripts

> *"I could give you kind of like a rough breakdown of how I think that they've got it
> structured, and you can just let me know if that sounds about right. So it's about
> $105,000 going to pay off the current balance, about $13,000 to pay off the
> forbearance, and then about $49,000 cash in hand. The rest would be the closing cost.
> Is that right?"*

**This is genuinely excellent.** He reverse-engineered a competitor's loan structure from
partial information, in real time, and confirmed it with the borrower.

This is what expertise sounds like. It's the single best moment across all four calls, and
it's the kind of thing that makes a borrower think *"this guy actually knows what he's
doing."*

### Thorough discovery
The most complete fact-gathering of the four: rate, payment, taxes, insurance breakdown,
loan amount, closing costs, employment, income structure, the gap, W-2 vs 1099, title vs
loan. Real work.

### The appraisal question was handled with partial honesty
> *"There's a couple different ways we can go about it. One could simply be to cancel the
> appraisal with PennyMac, and then we could reorder it through my company. Or you can
> continue on with the appraisal and simply have them transfer it over."*

The mechanics are correct. (The framing that follows is not — see below.)

### "Part ways as friends"
> *"If you like it, I'll send over a loan estimate. If not, we'll just part ways with
> friends."*

Good low-pressure close. Reduces resistance meaningfully.

## What went wrong

### ❌ 1. The sentence that should never have been said

> *"I've worked with over 50 different lenders but we definitely — **I know for a fact I
> can do a lot better than a 7.2.**"*

Said to a borrower with:
- A **581 FICO**
- A **$13,000 forbearance** still owed
- A **recent 12-week employment gap**
- **No credit pulled**
- **No pricing run**

**This is an unsubstantiated promise of a material loan term.** UDAAP-deceptive. A state
MLO conduct violation. And if it appeared in any marketing, a Reg N / MAP Rule problem.

It gets worse — 7.2% for a 581 FICO cash-out with a forbearance is **not obviously a bad
rate**. It may well be a fair market price for that risk profile. The originator doesn't
know, because he hasn't priced it.

**The real-world harm:** Tyler might cancel a working loan on the strength of that
promise. If the callback comes in at 7.4%, he's lost time, possibly his appraisal fee,
and possibly the deal.

**What should have been said:**

> *"7.2 is high for a 30-year fixed, but I want to be honest with you — with a 581 and a
> forbearance in the file, that might actually be a fair price. I don't know yet. What I
> can tell you is I've got fifty lenders and they don't all price credit the same way, so
> there's a real chance somebody's better. I'm not going to promise you a number before I
> pull credit. If I can't beat it, I'll tell you to stay with PennyMac."*

**Stronger, not weaker.** `../04-compliance/24-the-never-say-list.md`.

### ❌ 2. Disparaging the competitor with a speculative accusation

> *"That process tends to be a little bit more annoying because obviously PennyMac is
> going to want to maintain your business. **They're going to try not to send it over and
> just not comply.**"*

He's asserting that a named competitor will refuse to comply with a regulated process, with
no knowledge that they would. Deceptive disparagement, in service of getting Tyler to
cancel a paid appraisal and reorder through him.

**Note what's at stake for Tyler:** he's already paid for that appraisal. If he cancels
and this doesn't work out, he's out the money.

**The honest version:**

> *"Appraisals can transfer — it's allowed, though it takes some coordination and not
> every lender will accept one. Here's what I'd actually recommend: don't cancel anything
> on their side until we know my numbers genuinely beat theirs. I don't want you out an
> appraisal fee on a maybe."*

Protecting the borrower's money at your own expense is the strongest trust move available
to you. It was right there and it was passed over.

### ❌ 3. The borrower told him the answer and was talked past

This is the biggest **sales** failure in the call.

> Tyler: *"My goal right now is just to get the cash out as fast as possible, pay off my
> debt. And then **I'm planning on selling this house in the next two years** and moving.
> That's really why I'm not too worried about loans. That 7.3 doesn't really scare me
> because **I'm not going to ride out the entirety of the loan**, if that makes sense."*

Tyler just handed over his complete decision framework:
1. Speed matters most
2. He's selling in ~24 months
3. **Rate genuinely doesn't matter much to him** — and he's right

**The response:**

> *"No, I understand. I mean, one way or another, two years, if I can save you $3,000,
> $4,000, $5,000, that's just more money being put into your pockets rather than the
> banks."*

He acknowledged it and then kept selling on rate anyway.

**What a 2-year horizon actually implies:**

| Product | Why |
|---|---|
| **A 5/1 or 7/1 ARM** | Prices meaningfully below a 30-year fixed *precisely because* he's not keeping it 30 years. This is the textbook ARM borrower. |
| **A lender-credit structure** | He shouldn't pay points on a loan he's exiting in 24 months. Take a higher rate, get closing costs covered. |
| **Speed-optimized lender selection** | He said speed is the priority. That's a real differentiator and the originator had it — UWM turn times vs PennyMac's 30–45 days. |

**The ARM was never mentioned.** For a borrower who explicitly said he's selling in two
years, that's the single most appropriate product on the shelf.

He was sold on savings he didn't ask for, when he'd just explained he cared about speed.

### ❌ 4. The debt consolidation math was never run

> *"Just to cash out, to pay off some debt, pay off the car."*

That's the purpose — and unusually, it was actually asked and answered. Then nothing was
done with it.

**Never asked:**
- What are the balances?
- What are the rates?
- What are the monthly payments?
- What's the current **blended rate** across everything?

Without those you cannot demonstrate the value of consolidation, which is the entire
argument for this loan. `../03-underwriting/17-the-math-in-your-head.md`.

If Tyler's paying 24% on cards and 11% on a car, the blended-rate story is compelling and
completely unavailable without the numbers.

### ❌ 5. The forbearance was never explored

A **$13,000 forbearance** is enormously material. It affects:
- Which lenders will touch the file at all
- Seasoning and post-forbearance guideline requirements
- Whether the borrower has made the required consecutive post-forbearance payments
- Pricing

It was noted as a payoff line item and never discussed. **This is the item most likely to
kill the deal**, and it got less attention than the escrow breakdown.

### ❌ 6. The wife on title but not on the loan

> *"My wife is going to be on the title, but she's not going to be on the loan."*
> *"Not a problem. Okay, not a problem."*

There is usually a **reason** for that structure — commonly her credit, or her debts, or
income documentation. That reason is material.

It's also a mechanical issue: in community property states, a non-borrowing spouse's
debts can affect qualification on some products, and she'll need to sign certain documents
at closing regardless.

"Not a problem" twice is not a diagnosis.

### ❌ 7. "The minimum is actually going to be a 500"

> *"I mean the minimum is actually going to be a 500."*

Technically FHA's floor, practically misleading — 500 requires 10% down and virtually no
wholesale lender will buy it without heavy overlays. To a 581-FICO borrower this creates a
false sense of easy qualification.

## The call that should have happened

> *[after learning 581, forbearance, 7.2%]*
>
> *"Okay, that context helps a lot. Let me be honest with you — with a 581 and a
> forbearance in the file, 7.2 might actually be a fair price. I don't know yet, and I'm
> not going to promise you a number before I pull credit. What I can tell you is that
> fifty lenders don't price credit the same way, so there's a real chance somebody's
> better. If they're not, I'll tell you to stay with PennyMac."*
>
> *"Now — you said you're selling in two years. That changes what I'd recommend
> completely. Has anyone shown you an ARM? A 5/1 prices below a 30-year fixed exactly
> because you're not keeping it thirty years. You'd be paying for insurance you don't
> need with a fixed."*
>
> *"And you said the cash is for debt. Give me the balances and the rates on everything —
> cards, the car, all of it. If you're paying 24% on cards, the story here isn't the
> mortgage rate at all, it's what your total monthly outflow looks like on the other side."*
>
> *"Last thing — tell me about the forbearance. When did it end, and how many payments
> have you made since? That's the thing most likely to determine who'll do this loan, so
> I'd rather know now than in three weeks."*
>
> *"And don't cancel that appraisal. Let's see my numbers first — I don't want you out
> the fee on a maybe."*

## The four lessons

1. **Never promise to beat a rate before you've priced it.** Especially on damaged
   credit, where the competitor's number may be correct.
2. **When a borrower tells you their time horizon, change the product.** Two years means
   ARM and lender credits, not a 30-year fixed with points.
3. **"Pay off debt" is not a purpose — it's a category.** Get balances, rates, and
   payments or you can't make the argument.
4. **Never advise a borrower to cancel something they've already paid for** until you can
   deliver. Protecting their money is the strongest move you have.
