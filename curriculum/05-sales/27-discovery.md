# Discovery

Half the call. The stage that decides everything downstream. Also the stage new LOs
rush hardest, because collecting facts feels like progress and asking about feelings
feels like stalling.

It isn't.

---

## The core distinction

There are two kinds of information, and beginners collect only one.

| **Facts** | **Drivers** |
|---|---|
| Balance, value, rate, income, FICO | Why they want this, what happens if they don't, what they're afraid of |
| Needed for **qualification** | Needed for **the sale** |
| Get them from the borrower or the file | Only come from good questions |
| Underwriting cares | The borrower cares |

**The transcripts collect facts almost exclusively.** Call 1 asks nineteen consecutive
factual questions — balance, residence, cash out, payment, escrow, rate, term, veteran
status, disability, percentage, dollar amount, employment, tenure, income, W-2 status,
value. Textbook data collection.

Not once does he ask **why $120,000**. What's it for? What happens if he doesn't get it?
Is there a deadline? Has he been turned down before? Is his spouse on board?

He has enough to price a loan and nothing to sell one with.

> **The rule: facts qualify the loan. Drivers close the borrower. You need both, and you
> should get the drivers first** — because once you know the driver, you know which facts
> actually matter.

---

## The question that beats all others

> **"What would a successful outcome look like to you?"**

Victor's question, and it's the best single question in mortgage sales. Use it early.

Why it outperforms "what are you looking for":

- It asks for a **destination**, not a product. People describe products badly and
  outcomes well.
- It surfaces the **real** criterion. Someone says "lowest rate" and then describes
  success as "not having a $900 credit card payment anymore" — those are different sales.
- It hands you your **closing language**. Whatever they say here, you say back to them in
  Stage 5.
- It's **unexpected**, so it makes people think rather than recite.

**Variants for different call types:**

- Refi/cash-out: *"If this goes perfectly, what's different about your life in 60 days?"*
- Purchase: *"Walk me through what has to happen for this to be a win for you."*
- Competitor-locked: *"What would have to be true for you to switch?"*
- Hostile: *"What would make this worth two minutes of your time?"*

## The Question Stack

Run this order. It's built so each answer sets up the next.

### Layer 1 — Situation (30 sec)
Cheap, easy, non-threatening. Gets them talking.

- "How long have you been in the house?"
- "Is this your primary residence?"
- "Are you the only one on the loan, or is there someone else in this decision?"

*That last one prevents the "I need to talk to my spouse" objection two stages later. Ask
it here, every time.*

### Layer 2 — Purpose (the important one)
Why are we having this conversation at all?

- "What got you looking at this in the first place?"
- **"What would a successful outcome look like to you?"**
- "Is there a timeline, or are you just exploring?"
- "What would you do with the money?" *(cash-out — never skip this)*

### Layer 3 — Pain (where the sale lives)
The consequences of the status quo. Go one level deeper than feels comfortable.

- "What happens if this doesn't get done?"
- "How long has that been a problem?"
- "What have you already tried?"
- "What's that costing you every month right now?"

### Layer 4 — Facts (qualification)
Now collect. It's faster now, because they're invested and they know why you're asking.

- Balance, estimated value, current rate, loan type
- Income, employment type, tenure
- Credit estimate, any recent events
- Other debts if consolidating
- Taxes and insurance

### Layer 5 — Decision process
How this actually gets decided.

- "Who else weighs in on this?"
- "Are you talking with anyone else about it?" *(get this early — it's not an insult)*
- "What would make you say yes today?"
- "What's the thing most likely to stop this?"

## Explain the nosy questions

You're asking a stranger for their income, their debts, and their Social Security
number. That is objectively strange. Acknowledge it and it stops being strange.

**The pattern: question + why.**

| Question | The "why" that makes it land |
|---|---|
| "Are you receiving VA disability?" | "It's tax-free income, so underwriting grosses it up 25% — it counts for more than the number looks like." |
| "What's your credit look like?" | "Not judging it — score drives pricing in tiers, so a 20-point difference can be real money and I want to aim at the right tier." |
| "What are the balances on those cards?" | "If we're consolidating, the blended rate is the whole decision. I need the balances and the rates to tell you whether this actually helps." |
| "Any late payments in the last year?" | "One 30-day late in the last 12 months changes which lenders will touch this. Better we find it now than at week three." |
| "How long have you owned the business?" | "Two years is the line for most conventional programs. Under that, we're in different products — not worse, just different." |

Every one of those teaches them something. **Discovery done well is indistinguishable
from consulting.**

## What the transcripts missed

Real diagnostic value — these are the questions that were available and not asked.

### Call 1 — Michael, VA, 3.1%, wants $120k

Missed:
- **"What's the $120,000 for?"** — the single most important question in the call, never
  asked. Debt? Business? Home improvement? Medical? Each is a different loan and a
  different urgency.
- "Do you know what your rate would be on a cash-out today?" — sets expectations before
  the callback instead of after
- "Have you looked at a second mortgage that leaves the 3.1% alone?" — the actual right
  product
- "Is your wife part of this decision?"
- On the 30-day late: he was mid-explanation and got interrupted. **Let him talk.**

### Call 2 — Paula, hostile, wants $200k cash out

Missed:
- **"What's the $200,000 for?"** — again
- "You said the credit union quoted 6-something. Do you have that in writing?"
- She said *"maybe I'll be dead by the time it's due"* — a real, human, revealing
  statement. It was laughed past. That's an estate/timeline conversation and it's
  central to whether a 30-year term is right for her.
- Her 15-year loan at 2.3% is being replaced by a 30-year at ~7%. **Nobody did the total
  interest math with her.** That is a fiduciary-grade omission.

### Call 3 — Tyler, 581, forbearance, wants $49k

Missed:
- **"What's the debt you're paying off, and at what rates?"** — this is a debt
  consolidation deal and the blended-rate math was never run
- "You said you're selling in two years — have you priced what this costs you over 24
  months versus what it saves you?" *(He raised it himself. It was waved away.)*
- "Tell me about the forbearance" — hugely material to which lenders will touch this
- "What's your wife's income?" — she's on title, not on the loan. Why? Sometimes that's a
  credit problem worth knowing about.

### Call 4 — Fasani, self-employed trucking, wants $100k

Missed:
- **"When will the returns actually be filed?"** — this is the gate on the entire deal
- "What do your business bank statements look like monthly?" — the bank-statement
  program is the actual answer here and it was never raised
- "What's the $100k expansion — trucks, drivers, a contract?" — determines urgency and
  timeline
- "Is the business an LLC, S-corp, sole prop?" — completely changes income calculation
- The 30-day late from a hacked account: "Do you have the bank's letter?" A documented
  bank error can sometimes be disputed or explained to underwriting.

**Pattern across all four: nobody asked what the money was for.** Every single call was a
cash-out request, and in every single one the *purpose* — the thing that determines
urgency, product fit, and whether the deal is even good for the borrower — went
uncollected.

## Listen and acknowledge

Victor's third tip, and the mechanical version:

**Acknowledge with content, not with noise.** "Okay, beautiful" is noise. It appears
eleven times in Call 1 and proves nothing was heard.

**The pattern: reflect + advance.**

> Borrower: *"I was on paternity leave for 12 weeks, so I didn't have four pay stubs when
> I first applied."*
>
> ❌ *"Okay, gotcha. Not a problem."*
>
> ✅ *"So the gap cost you the timing — you had a 6.1 available and by the time you were
> back with enough stubs it had moved to 7.2. That's rough. Is that the whole reason the
> rate jumped, or did something else change?"*

The second one proves you were listening, shows you understood the *implication*, and
asks a better question. That is what makes someone feel heard.

## Discovery discipline

- **Take notes.** Quote them back later. It's the most powerful thing in the call.
- **Don't solve mid-discovery.** The instant you start explaining a product, discovery is
  over. Hold it.
- **Silence after hard questions.** Especially about money, credit, and family.
- **Follow the emotion.** When their voice changes — that's the thread. Pull it.
- **One question at a time.** Stacked questions get you an answer to the last one only.
- **Never interrupt a disclosure.** Late payments, job changes, divorces, bankruptcies.
  That information is expensive and they're giving it away.

---

## What you must be able to do

- Distinguish facts from drivers, and explain why drivers come first
- Run the five-layer stack from memory
- Attach a "why" to every sensitive question
- Ask what the money is for — every single cash-out call, without exception
- Reflect and advance instead of acknowledging with filler

**Self-check:** A borrower wants $80,000 cash out. You have their balance, value, rate,
and income. What are the three most important questions you haven't asked?

> *1. What's the $80,000 for? 2. What happens if you don't get it — is there a deadline
> or a consequence? 3. Is anyone else part of this decision? Without those you can price
> a loan but you cannot sell one, and you don't know whether it's even the right product.*
