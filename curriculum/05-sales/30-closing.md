# Closing

> *"Closing isn't about pressuring people. It's about making the decision feel natural
> and logical."*

The four closes below come from the source material. Each is presented with the ethical
line that keeps it usable — because three of the four have a common form that crosses
into deception, and that form is what most people actually copy.

---

## Before any technique: ask

The most common reason a mortgage call doesn't close is that **nobody asked**.

New LOs present numbers, hear "okay, let me think," say "sounds good, I'll follow up,"
and hang up. No close was ever attempted. The call didn't fail — it never happened.

**Every call gets a close.** Even a bad one. Even a rejected one.

---

## The micro-close principle

You are not closing "the loan." You are closing **the next small step**. A borrower who
won't commit to a mortgage will readily commit to a credit pull.

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Do you want to move forward?" | "Let's get credit pulled so I can firm this up — five minutes?" |
| "Should we do this?" | "Send me the last two paystubs and I'll have the LE out today." |
| "What do you think?" | "I'll call you at 4:15 to go through the numbers. Good?" |

Big asks get "let me think about it." Small asks get "sure."

## 1. The takeaway close

**Mechanism:** people want what they might lose. Remove the pressure and you remove the
resistance.

**The scenario:** *"I got a lower quote from another lender. I like you, but I have to do
what's best for my family."*

**The good part of the source version:**

> *"Thank you for not ghosting me and for bringing me another option — I appreciate the
> diligence, and honestly I love how smart you're being. I'd do exactly the same. Let me
> ask you this: outside of rate, have I done a good job for you so far?"*
> **[silence — let them affirm]**

That's excellent. It affirms them, it's genuine, and the question extracts a verbal
commitment about your value that is independent of price.

**⚠️ Where the source version goes wrong:**

> ❌ *"There are times my upper management will match another lender's offer if I request
> it. I know I can get the approval — but they'll only do it if we lock today and get the
> appraisal initiated."*

If no such policy exists, that's fabricated authority plus fabricated urgency, both
material. See `../04-compliance/24-the-never-say-list.md`.

**The honest version, which works as well or better:**

> *"Todd, you matter to me and I worked hard on this pre-approval. Here's exactly how my
> pricing works: I have discretion within my comp structure, and I'd rather spend it
> keeping your business than lose you over an eighth. Send me their Loan Estimate — the
> real form. If it genuinely beats mine, I'll tell you to take it, and I'll mean it. If I
> can beat it, can I have your commitment that we move forward today?"*

**The true takeaway** — the strongest version of this close — is simply:

> *"Honestly? If their number's better, take it. I'd tell my own brother the same thing."*

Said with genuine indifference, this closes more deals than any pressure tactic ever
will. It only works if you mean it, which is why pipeline abundance is a *closing*
strategy.

## 2. The urgency close

**Mechanism:** real deadlines move people.

**The rule: the urgency must be true.** In this market you have plenty of real urgency —
you never need to invent any.

**Real urgency available to you right now:**

| True urgency | Why it's real |
|---|---|
| "Rates moved 18 bps yesterday; my lock desk reprices intraday" | Verifiably true in Aug 2026 |
| "Your rate lock expires Thursday" | Contractual |
| "Your purchase contract's financing contingency is the 15th" | Contractual, and real money at risk |
| "This lender's program is being retired at month-end" | Happens constantly in wholesale |
| "Your pre-approval expires in 30 days, credit's already pulled" | Documentable |
| "Your quote's built on a 720; if the card balances report higher next cycle you tier down" | Genuinely mechanical |

> *"I watch pricing daily and it's been moving against us. If we lock today you've got
> this. If we wait and it moves, I have to come back with a different number and I don't
> want that conversation. What do you want to do?"*

**❌ Fabricated urgency to never use:** "this rate is only good for the next hour," "my
manager's approval expires today," "I only have one slot left." All deceptive, all
discoverable, all career risk.

## 3. The assumptive close

**Mechanism:** don't ask *whether* — describe *what happens next*.

> *"Okay Todd, the 30-year fixed lands right where we wanted the payment. I like it for
> you. Let's move forward — here's what happens next. I'll send the disclosures in about
> ten minutes, they come by e-sign. Get those back to me today and I'll have the
> appraisal ordered tomorrow morning."*

Note the mechanics:
- **"We"** and **"us"**, not "you"
- **"I like it for you"** — a recommendation, which is what advisors give
- **No question mark.** The decision is treated as made.
- Immediately concrete next actions

**This is the highest-percentage close in mortgage** and it's the one new LOs are most
afraid to use, because it feels presumptuous. It isn't — it's what confidence sounds
like. A borrower who doesn't want to proceed will simply say so.

**The ethical line:** assumptive framing is fine. Assumptive *action* is not. Never
process an application, pull credit, or order an appraisal without explicit
authorization. Assume the decision in your language, never in your file.

---

## 4. The swap-out close

**Mechanism:** when they're hesitant about the offer, change the offer rather than
pushing it.

> *"Todd, you're three years into a 30-year. Instead of refinancing back into another
> thirty, let's look at a twenty-five. You still cut the monthly payment, but you don't
> give back the three years you've already paid, and you save real money in long-term
> interest. Can I show you the math on both?"*

This is the most consultative of the four and the most durable. It works because it
responds to an actual concern with an actual structural change.

**Swap-outs available to you:**

| Their hesitation | The swap |
|---|---|
| "I don't want to restart 30 years" | 25-year or 20-year term |
| "I don't want to lose my 3% rate" | **Second lien instead of cash-out refi** |
| "Closing costs are too high" | Lender credit at a slightly higher rate |
| "Payment's still too high" | Longer term, or buy down the rate |
| "I don't want PMI" | 80/10/10, or lender-paid MI |
| "I'm selling in two years" | ARM instead of a 30-year fixed |

That second row is the defining swap of this market. Learn it cold.

## 5. The summary close (added — not in the source material)

Underused and extremely effective, especially with analytical borrowers.

> *"Let me make sure I've got this right. You need $120,000 for the business. You want the
> payment under $2,000. You've got a 3.1% you don't want to lose, and the returns won't
> be filed until March. So here's what I'd do: a fixed second for the $120k, which leaves
> your 3.1% completely alone, and we use bank statements so the tax returns don't gate
> it. Payment lands around $1,850. Did I miss anything?"*

Every element is **their own stated requirement**. "Did I miss anything?" is a close
disguised as a comprehension check — and it's almost impossible to answer with a vague
"let me think about it," because you've asked a specific question.

---

## 6. The alternative-choice close

Give two options, both of which are yes.

> *"Do you want to do the credit pull now, or should I call you back at four when you're
> off the job?"*
> *"Should I run this as a 30-year or do you want to see the 25 too?"*

**Use it lightly.** Sophisticated borrowers recognize it, and it can feel manipulative if
the alternatives are artificial. It works best when both options are genuinely useful.

---

## The ethical spine

Three of the four source closes share a shape: **create a constraint that doesn't exist**
— fake approval, fake deadline, fake scarcity.

That shape is what most people copy, and it's what gets licenses revoked
(`../04-compliance/23-your-license-and-career.md`).

**The test, before any close:**

> *If the borrower could see everything I know right now, would they feel this was fair?*

If yes, close hard — you're doing them a service by moving them past hesitation. If no,
you're not closing, you're manipulating, and it will eventually cost you far more than
the deal is worth.

The genuinely good news: **the honest versions convert as well or better.** "If theirs is
better, take it" outperforms a fake manager approval, because borrowers have heard the
fake one before and they've never heard the real one.

---

## After the close

**If yes:**
- Confirm the next three steps out loud, specifically
- Send something within ten minutes — momentum is perishable
- Book the next contact before hanging up

**If no:**
- Find out why: *"Totally fine — what would have had to be different?"*
- Ask for the future: *"If your situation changes, can I check in in ninety days?"*
- Log it. A no today is frequently a yes in six months, and a well-maintained "no"
  database is one of the most profitable assets in this business.

**If maybe:**
- That's a no wearing a disguise. Use the silence escalation
  (`29-objection-mastery.md`) and get to the real answer. A clean no is worth far more
  than a maybe, because a maybe consumes your follow-up capacity indefinitely.

---

## What you must be able to do

- Close every call, without exception
- Close the next small step, not the whole loan
- Deliver an assumptive close without a question mark
- Identify a real urgency in the current market and use it
- Run the fairness test before deploying any close

**Self-check:** You presented numbers, they said "this looks good, let me sleep on it."
What do you say?

> *"Of course — sleeping on it is smart. Let me do one thing so you're sleeping on real
> information instead of a memory: I'll get credit pulled now so tomorrow's number is
> the actual number and not an estimate. Takes five minutes, and if you decide against it
> you've lost nothing. Then I'll call you at ten tomorrow. Fair?"*
>
> *You've honored the request, advanced the file, and booked the next contact. The
> decision is still theirs — which is exactly as it should be.*
