# Sales First Principles

Everything else in this folder is an application of what's in this document. Read it
until you can teach it.

---

## 1. The core reframe

> **Sales is not about convincing. It is about positioning.**

Amir Syed's line, and it's the correct starting point. Here's what it actually means.

**Convincing** assumes the borrower is on the wrong side of a wall and your job is to
drag them over. It's adversarial, it's exhausting, and it produces the behavior that
makes cold calling miserable — pushing, repeating yourself, arguing.

**Positioning** assumes the borrower will make a rational decision if they can see the
situation clearly, and your job is to arrange what they can see. You're not moving the
person. You're moving the frame.

Concrete version. A borrower says *"I want to wait for rates to drop."*

- **Convincing:** "Rates aren't going to drop, you should act now." *(You are now arguing
  about a fact neither of you knows. You cannot win this.)*
- **Positioning:** "Say you're right and they drop a full point next year. Two things
  happen — your payment drops about $180, and roughly five million more buyers come back
  into the market and start bidding against you. Which of those two moves more money?"
  *(You didn't argue. You added the variable they weren't looking at.)*

The second one works because you never asked them to be wrong.

## 2. You are the product

Amir made 50,000+ cold calls with nothing but a name, an address, a rate, and a phone
number. No body language, no visuals, no brand. His conclusion:

> *"I didn't need better leads or a better product. I just needed to learn how to
> communicate at a higher level, because I was the product."*

This is literally true in mortgage, more than in almost any other sales job, for a
structural reason:

**Money is a commodity.** As a broker you have 50+ lenders. So does the guy who's calling
your borrower right now. You will frequently be selling *the exact same loan from the
exact same lender* as your competitor. The note rate is set by the secondary market, not
by you (`01-industry/02-follow-the-money.md`).

So what actually differs between you and the other caller?

- Whether you understood their situation
- Whether you told them something true they didn't know
- Whether they believe this will close on time
- Whether they *like* you

That's it. That's the whole product. Which is good news — every one of those is
trainable, and rate isn't.

### The corollary that kills excuses

> *"I had all the typical limiting beliefs — if I just had better leads, or if I just had
> a better product."*

Every struggling LO has an external explanation. Leads are bad. Rates are high. The
market is slow. Some of it is even true. None of it is *actionable*, which is what makes
it poison. Ruthlessly convert every complaint into a variable you control:

| Complaint | The controllable version |
|---|---|
| "These leads are garbage" | "My contact rate is 4%. What would get it to 8%?" |
| "Rates are too high" | "I'm selling rate. What if I sold monthly cash flow?" |
| "They ghosted me" | "I ended the call without a specific time. Fix that." |
| "Nobody's buying" | "How many people did I actually talk to yesterday?" |

## 3. Stop selling, start helping

Zig Ziglar's line, and Victor's first tip. It sounds like a poster in a break room. It's
actually a mechanical instruction.

**People don't want to be sold. They want their problem solved.** When you focus on the
sale, they can hear it — in your pacing, in which questions you skip, in how you respond
to a detail that's inconvenient for you. When you focus on the problem, they can hear
that too.

The operational test, on every call:

> **Am I trying to find out whether I can help this person, or am I trying to get them
> into an application?**

Those produce different questions and radically different outcomes.

**The paradox that makes this work:** the single most persuasive thing you can say on a
mortgage call is *"you shouldn't do this."* Nobody expects it. It proves your
recommendations mean something. And it costs you almost nothing, because the deal you
talk someone out of was usually a deal that was going to fall apart in underwriting
anyway.

> *"I have a personal policy to never convince anyone to refinance when it doesn't make
> sense."* — and Amir's point is that he means it, because he generates more leads than
> he can work. **Abundance is what makes non-desperation possible.** If you're starving,
> the borrower hears it. Which is a business-development argument, not a mindset
> argument: fix your pipeline and your tonality fixes itself.

## 4. The three skills that control every conversation

### Skill 1 — Tonality

> *"How you say something is even more important than what you say."*

People don't buy words, they buy how words make them feel. Weak, uncertain, or desperate
tonality kills a call regardless of content. Confident, controlled, patient tonality
carries mediocre content a surprisingly long way.

This is the single biggest gap in coaching you from transcripts — text has no tonality.
Full treatment in `28-tonality-lab.md`.

### Skill 2 — Sequencing

> *"The order in which you say things matters most."*

The canonical example: **low-level LOs discuss rate at the beginning. High-level LOs
discuss it at the end.**

Why sequencing works — three reasons, and understanding them lets you generalize:

1. **Information given before context is worthless.** "6.875%" means nothing to someone
   who doesn't know what drives it or what they qualify for. You've spent your only card
   and bought nothing.
2. **Value must precede price.** Universal in sales. Quote before establishing value and
   the number gets compared to nothing but other numbers.
3. **Whoever asks the questions controls the conversation.** Answering their opening
   question hands them the wheel. Full arc in `26-the-call-arc.md`.

### Skill 3 — Positioning

> *"How you position the conversation controls the outcome."*

The example worth memorizing. Borrower asks *"what's your rate?"*

- ❌ Amateur: *"6.875%."* — you're now a price on a list
- ✅ Pro: *"Are you looking purely for the lowest rate, or for the best overall financing
  strategy?"*

That question does an enormous amount of work in eleven words. It implies those are
different things (they are). It implies you know the difference (you do). It moves you
from vendor to advisor. And whichever they answer, you've learned what actually drives
their decision.

**The general form:** when asked a question that reduces you to a commodity, respond with
a question that reframes the category.

## 5. Objections are buying signals

> *"Objections aren't rejections. They're buying signals."*

The logic is airtight: **a person with zero interest hangs up.** They don't argue with
you about rates or explain that they're already working with someone. Engagement of any
kind — including hostile engagement — is engagement.

Look at Call 2. She opens with "I'm not interested," then argues, then complains about
her credit union's 6%, then gives her property value, her balance, her taxes, her
insurance, and books a callback. Her "not interested" was the least informative thing she
said.

**Reframe:** an objection is the borrower telling you exactly what stands between you and
the deal. That's free diagnostic information most people have to guess at.

> **But — and this is where the source material is dangerously incomplete — "objections
> are buying signals" is a claim about *sales psychology*, not a legal permission slip.**
> Some objections are revocations. "Take me off your list" is not a buying signal; it is
> an instruction with statutory force behind it. See `04-compliance/19-cold-calling-law.md`.
> Know the difference cold, because the coaching content everywhere in this industry
> blurs it.

## 6. Prevention beats handling

The best objection handling is the objection that never occurs. If you're getting the
same objection on 60% of calls, that's not an objection problem — that's a **script
defect upstream**.

| Recurring objection | What to fix earlier |
|---|---|
| "I need to talk to my spouse" | Ask in discovery: "Is anyone else part of this decision?" |
| "I need to think about it" | You didn't build enough urgency or enough value |
| "Your rate is too high" | You quoted before establishing value |
| "How did you get my number?" | Your opening wasn't honest about the source |
| "I'm already working with someone" | Expect it — it's the default state, have the reframe ready |

This is Amir's **preemptive strike** and it's the highest-leverage technique in the whole
system. Full treatment in `29-objection-mastery.md`.

## 7. Silence

> *"Most salespeople talk too damn much."*

Silence does something no sentence can: it creates pressure without applying any. Humans
are compelled to fill it. When you ask a real question and then shut up, what comes back
is usually more honest than anything you'd have gotten by prompting.

**The rule:** after you ask a closing question or a hard question, **say nothing until
they speak.** Not "does that make sense?" Not a clarification. Nothing.

This is physically uncomfortable for about three seconds and it is worth practicing
until it isn't. Real example of the cost — Call 1:

> Borrower: *"...he was like, well, hang on, let me look because I haven't been late in
> the last two years—"*
> LO: *"Still with me, Michael? Are you there?"*

The borrower was mid-disclosure on the most important underwriting fact in the call. The
right move was silence. He filled it instead and the thread was lost.

## 8. Inclusive language

Small, cheap, works.

| Instead of | Say |
|---|---|
| "You should do a 25-year" | "**We** should look at a 25-year" |
| "You'll need to send me" | "**Let's** get me" |
| "Your file" | "**Our** file" |

"We" puts you on the same side of the table. This is what an advisor sounds like and
it's most of the difference between "salesperson" and "my mortgage guy."

## 9. The mental model to carry

You are not a loan officer. **You are a people officer.**

> *"The more people who see you, know you, like you, trust you, and love you, the more
> loans you'll close."*

And the durable version of this, worth taping to your monitor:

> **If you looked at the top 100 loan officers in the country, most of them do not have
> the lowest rates. They have the best people, product, and process skills.**

If the lowest rate won, the rankings would be a list of whoever priced worst that
quarter. They aren't. They're a list of the best communicators.

## What you must be able to do

- Explain positioning vs. convincing, with an example
- Explain why sequencing puts rate at the end
- Reframe "what's your rate?" without answering it
- Explain why an objection is information, and where that framing legally stops
- Stay silent after asking a hard question

**Self-check:** *"I'm just shopping for the lowest rate."* Reframe it without arguing.

> *"Smart — I'd do the same. Just so I'm shopping for the right thing on your behalf: if
> a slightly lower rate came with higher closing costs, a longer close, and a lender who
> put your earnest money at risk by missing the contingency date, would that still be the
> best deal? Or do you want lowest total cost with a close that actually happens?"*
